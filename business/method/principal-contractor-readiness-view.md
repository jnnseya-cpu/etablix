# The principal contractor's readiness assessment — the buyer's view, not ours

## Read this first

**ETABLIX is not, and must never appear to be, the CDM 2015 Principal
Contractor.** The professional indemnity and public liability policy in force
insures a consultant — "Consultant — building", "Consultant — procurement" —
and does not respond to holding works contracts. Most of the organisations this
business sells to hold Principal Contractor and Principal Designer themselves,
on their own sites, and would read any suggestion otherwise as either a
misunderstanding of the role or an attempt to compete with them.

This document is written from the principal contractor's chair, and the voice
has to be read past rather than copied. But it was first filed here as nothing
more than the customer's motive, and that was too narrow a reading.

**The body of work it describes is work ETABLIX does — across all three
delivery models.** Site control, establishment as a managed package, layouts
tested against production, operational authority, the findings register, the
four commercial questions, the evidence behind a release: that is the job. What
changes between the three models is who holds the contracts, who directs the
suppliers and who pays them. What never changes is that **the Principal
Contractor appointment is the client's and stays the client's**, including
under Model C, where "prime" means prime for the site-services system only and
nothing beyond it.

So: read the content as the work, and the voice as the client's. Where it says
"as principal contractor, I would require", the correct reading here is "this
is what our client must be able to evidence, and here is the part of it we
produce for them, under whichever model they have appointed us on".

The section below maps it. The companion document,
`pre-construction-site-assessment.md`, is the pre-construction method itself.

## Why it is worth having anyway

Three things in it are directly useful.

**Section 5 is ETABLIX's product, described by the person who should be buying
it.** "Treat site setup as a construction package… It needs a scope, design
inputs, responsibilities, procurement, inspections, programme and acceptance
criteria." That is the Advisory deliverable, written in the buyer's own words
and from the buyer's own motive. It is worth more as a sentence a client says
than as a sentence we say.

**Section 9 is the commercial argument in four questions.** What information is
missing, who provides it, when it is needed, and what happens to cost and
programme if it is not resolved. A principal contractor who cannot answer those
four about site establishment is carrying unpriced risk — and that is the
whole case for buying the specification rather than improvising it.

**The point about the first workers is the sharpest line in either document.**
The people installing the compound need welfare and safe arrangements before
the compound exists. The completed compound does not solve the conditions
during its own installation. Almost nobody prices that, and it is a specific,
checkable observation that proves whoever says it has done the work.

## What the principal contractor is assessing

| Assessment | Their question |
|---|---|
| Permanent works buildability | Can we construct this design safely and in a workable sequence? |
| Temporary construction infrastructure | What access, platforms, temporary works, utilities, welfare and logistics do we need? |
| Site control and interfaces | How will we control subcontractors, neighbouring activity, live operations and the public? |

The second row is where ETABLIX sells. The first and third are theirs.

A site visit does not transfer every unknown condition to the contractor, and
missing information does not justify proceeding on an unsafe assumption.

## The visits, and when a decision can still change

| Visit | Purpose | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Tender | Test scope, access, methods, constraints, preliminary allowances | Tender queries, assumptions, exclusions, preliminary logistics plan |
| Post-award validation | Reconcile tender assumptions against actual requirements | Agreed actions, investigations, revised delivery strategy |
| Pre-mobilisation | Can establishment and enabling works proceed? | Readiness review, release of defined activities |
| Specialist package | Validate piling, lifting, demolition, utilities methods | Package design inputs and interface agreements |
| Before a phase change | Confirm the site is still suitable as workfronts and risks move | Revised layout, access and control arrangements |

**The tender visit is where ETABLIX is most useful and least often asked.**
Everything at that visit becomes a preliminary allowance, and the allowance
becomes the number. Attendance is not proof that every condition was
discoverable — record what could be inspected, what was inaccessible and what
information was unavailable.

Drawings alone are not sufficient for underground services: plan, locate and
identify, then excavate safely.

## Site setup as a construction package

Managed with the same discipline as foundations or steelwork — scope, design
inputs, responsibilities, procurement, inspections, programme, acceptance
criteria. The package covers:

possession, fencing, hoarding, security · access roads, crossings, turning,
unloading · ground preparation and cabin foundations · welfare, offices,
drying, induction · temporary electricity, water, drainage, communications ·
lighting, fire, emergency access · storage, waste, fuel, environmental controls
· maintenance, changes, demobilisation, reinstatement

**And the establishment of the establishment.** The first workers installing
the compound need welfare and safe arrangements of their own. Welfare is
required from the start of construction, not from the day the cabins are
commissioned.

## Layout tested against production

Before approving a compound or logistics layout, walk the sequence: where does
each major delivery arrive, how is it unloaded, where is it stored, how does it
reach its installation position, what plant and people movement does that
create, what must stop while it happens, and does emergency access survive it?

*Steel laydown beside the entrance looks convenient and may mean repeated
transport across excavations to reach the crane. A different laydown position,
or a different delivery sequence, removes the handling entirely.*

Phase layouts: enabling (access, welfare, drainage, segregation) · groundworks
(excavations, spoil, haul roads, piling platforms) · structure (crane
positions, assembly, laydown) · fit-out (loading, internal distribution,
protection of completed areas) · commissioning (energised systems, controlled
access, operational interfaces) · demobilisation (removal routes,
reinstatement).

## On a live site, authority before dates

Agree with the operator who controls each area, who authorises access and work,
who isolates and verifies, who approves interruptions, **who can stop work**,
and who accepts the installation back into operation.

The construction boundary does not contain the consequences of the work. Dust,
vibration, drainage changes, fire-system impairment and service interruption
reach the wider facility.

Before a shutdown: a readiness review covering design, materials, personnel,
isolation, installation, testing, restoration and rollback. A permit supports
those arrangements; it does not replace the risk assessment.

## A finding that can control delivery

> Reference → location → evidence → uncertainty → consequence → action → owner
> → deadline → closure evidence

> **LOG-014:** Proposed crane standing area includes an existing underground
> chamber. Chamber construction and load capacity unknown. Current crane
> arrangement cannot be released. Temporary works lead to coordinate
> investigation and revised platform design before crane mobilisation.

against *"check crane area"*, which drives nothing.

Keep observed, reported and verified distinct. Record drawing revisions and the
limits of the inspection.

## Four questions that turn a constraint into a commercial position

1. What information or decision is missing?
2. Who is responsible for providing it?
3. When is it needed, to avoid affecting the works?
4. What happens to cost and programme if it is not resolved?

| Constraint | Action | Delivery implication |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary power connection date unconfirmed | Obtain confirmed connection programme | Generation, fuel, maintenance, changeover |
| Ground beneath haul road unknown | Investigate and complete design | Road construction and heavy deliveries await release |
| Existing drain crosses new foundation | Verify route, agree diversion or protection | Foundation sequence may change |
| Operational shutdown not approved | Agree window and acceptance criteria | Tie-in date stays conditional |

Allocate responsibility using the actual contract. Nothing about a survey, a
diversion or an unforeseen condition automatically belongs to either party.

## The release decision

A documented readiness review confirming, for the specific activities being
released: adequate information and suitable designs · available work areas and
controlled interfaces · safe access, welfare and emergency arrangements ·
suitable temporary works and plant · competent supervision and subcontractor
coordination · necessary permissions and operational approvals · a construction
phase plan appropriate to the actual risks.

The construction phase plan is required before construction begins, and
temporary works deserve the same care as permanent works.

**The report ends in a decision: what may proceed, what stays restricted, and
what evidence releases the next activity.**

Three rows carry the whole commercial argument and should be read together:
**who holds the contracts**, **who pays the suppliers**, and **who makes the
release decision**. Under A and B the client holds every contract and pays
every supplier, and we never touch their supply-chain money. Under all three,
the release decision and the CDM appointments stay with the client. That is not
modesty; it is where they lawfully sit, and a supplier who offers to take them
on is telling you they do not understand the regime.

**Model C may be described and planned for internally. It must not be offered,
quoted or implied to a prospect or client** until insurance is in place that
answers it: the policy in force insures a consultant and does not respond to
holding works contracts. The column above exists so the model is designed
properly before it is sellable, not so it can be sold early.

That is no longer only a rule somebody has to remember. The table above is now
data in the platform — `DELIVERY_MODELS` in `shared/constants.js` — and one
flag on the Model C record drives every control: the fee quotation cannot offer
it, `POST /api/docs/generate` refuses a document that names it, an engagement
cannot be opened under it, and the public pages, the deck and the outbound
letters all state that it is not open for appointment and why. Each of the
six deliverables that names a contracting party now asks which model the
appointment is under and writes from the answer; with no model recorded the
agent is forbidden from stating who contracts and must leave it as an open
client decision. When cover exists that answers Model C, `offerable` changes in
that one file and the controls follow. Nothing else needs editing.

## Where the sale sits today

The principal contractor owns the release decision and the accountability
attached to it. What they frequently do not have, at the moment they need it,
is the **evidence base for the site-establishment part of it** — the scope, the
capacities, the phased layouts, the temporary works register, the utilities
demand, the removal obligations.

Under Advisory we produce exactly that and they do the rest. Under Management
Integrator we also run the enquiry, the mobilisation and the interfaces, while
they still hold every contract and we still never hold their money. Either way
the decision stays theirs.
