# LinkedIn — Jonathan Berry, Project Director, HS2 Ltd

Handle this one differently from the others, and the difference matters.

**HS2 Ltd is the client, not a contractor.** Jonathan sits on the employer's
side of a publicly funded programme. He does not buy site services from a
small supplier — the main works civils contractors do, under contracts HS2
lets through public procurement. An approach that reads as a sales pitch to a
public official is ineffective at best; at worst it is the kind of thing a
programme director has to be seen to keep at arm's length, particularly if
anything relevant is in procurement.

So the message asks for **an opinion and a route**, offers nothing, and
explicitly does not ask him to introduce you to anybody. That is not
squeamishness — it is what makes it answerable. A question he can reply to
without checking with anyone is a question that gets a reply.

He is NEC-registered and MAPM. He thinks in NEC terms and in programme terms.
Write in that register and skip the marketing vocabulary entirely.

---

## The message

> Thanks for connecting, Jonathan.
>
> I'll keep this short and I'm not asking you for anything commercial — you
> are on the client side and I would not put you in that position.
>
> My background is construction subcontract management at GE Vernova, on EPC
> and turnkey projects across the UK, Ireland and Europe where the civils, the
> site management and the site infrastructure sat inside our own contract. I
> have since set up ETABLIX in Birmingham to do that one piece of work
> properly: site establishment and the serviced environment around a
> project — compound, temporary utilities, welfare, logistics, accommodation —
> planned and run as one accountable package rather than as separate orders
> with the gaps between them belonging to nobody.
>
> Two questions I would genuinely value your view on, and a one-line answer to
> either is plenty:
>
> On a linear programme at your scale, where does site establishment actually
> hurt — is it the consents and utility connections nobody worked backwards
> from, the welfare and accommodation sized against a headcount that then
> moved, or the interfaces between packages that each assumed somebody else
> had it?
>
> And, separately, what is the right route these days for a small specialist
> to be visible to the main works contractors — the published supply-chain
> portals, or directly to the tier ones? I would rather go the proper way
> round than the quick way.
>
> Either way, good to be connected, and thank you for the work — I have
> followed the programme with more interest than most.

---

## Why it is shaped like that

**"I'm not asking you for anything commercial" is the line that makes it
work.** It removes the thing he would otherwise have to manage, and it is
true. A director on a public programme reads twenty approaches a week that do
not say it.

**The first question is an expert-opinion question with no commercial edge.**
It costs him nothing to answer, it is interesting to him, and his answer is
worth more to you than a meeting would be — it tells you which of the three
failure modes a client at that scale actually loses money on, in his words.

**The second question asks for a route, not a favour.** How a supplier reaches
the tier ones is public information, so answering it is not a courtesy he has
to weigh. Asking him to introduce you to one of his contractors would be, and
it is the reason to keep it out.

---

## If he replies

Answer the substance first, at length if he engaged seriously. Do not convert
his answer into a pitch — the whole value of this contact is that he is a
source of judgement about how major programmes actually fail, and there is a
version of you in two years for whom that is worth far more than a job.

**Never mention a live HS2 procurement, a specific package, or a named
contractor's commercial position.** If he raises one, keep your reply general.

**If he offers something** — a name, a route, a suggestion — take it, thank
him plainly, and report back once you have acted on it. People who give advice
remember the ones who came back and said what happened, and almost nobody
does.

## If he does not reply

That is the likely outcome and it is not a snub. A project director on a
public programme has reasons to answer nothing that arrives unprompted. Leave
it. Do not follow up, do not re-send, and do not ask again in three weeks — on
this one contact, patience is the whole strategy. Post good work publicly and
let him see it.
