The second touch

Three people, contacted last week, none replied. That is normal and it is not a rejection — a first cold approach is read by roughly one person in four and answered by fewer. The follow-up is where the reply actually comes from, and only if it does a different job from the first one.

The four rules this is written to

Every one of these is the difference between a follow-up that gets answered and one that gets deleted with the first.

  1. Never repeat the first message. If they did not want it on Tuesday they do not want it again on the following Tuesday. The second message earns its place by carrying something the first one did not.
  2. Shrink the ask. The first message asked for a conversation. The second asks for one line — a category name, a route, a month. People answer questions they can answer in eight seconds.
  3. Give them the exit. An explicit “not now” option raises reply rates rather than lowering them, because it removes the fear that answering starts a pursuit.
  4. Reply in the original thread. Same subject with Re:, no new subject line, no attachment. An attachment from an unrecognised sender is the fastest route to a spam folder, so offer the document — never attach it.

One safeguard: if the Mark on the sourcing team and the Mark who is construction director are the same person, do not send both. Send the LinkedIn message only, and drop him from the email. A same-day double-touch from a stranger reads as pressure.

Before you send
  • Fill in the month in “try us in March” — make it a real one, roughly two quarters out.
  • If the first contact went to Martin and Mark as two separate emails, send two separate follow-ups on different days. A blast that was individual last week and joint this week tells them it was never personal.

Why it works: question 3 is not a sales question, it is the question a good sourcing manager already worries about. Answering it costs them nothing and starts the conversation on their ground rather than yours.

Mark — I messaged last week and got no reply, which is fair enough. No need to go back to it.

One question instead, because you will have a view and I would rather not guess at it.

On your sites, is the temporary set-up — accommodation, welfare, catering, utilities, security, transport — bought as one package, or split across several contracts?

Nothing to sell in this message. If your answer is “split, and it works fine”, I would genuinely like to hear why. It is the case I hear argued least often and I would rather understand it than assume it is wrong.

Justin
Rules for this one
  • No link, no attachment, no deck. A link in a first DM halves the reply rate and the message is strong enough without one.
  • If he replies with anything at all, do not pitch back. Answer his point, then ask for twenty minutes.
  • If he disagrees with you, that is the best outcome available. An argument is a relationship; silence is not.

3 · The third touch, and when to stop

Two more weeks. If the second message produces nothing, one more goes out and then you stop — and the one that stops is, reliably, the one that gets answered.

Then genuinely stop
  • Log all three contacts as an account in CONSTRUX with the dates, so the next approach in six months starts from a record and not from memory.
  • Set the six-month reminder the day you send this, not the day they fail to reply.
  • Keep going on the formal route in parallel. A supplier registration does not need anyone to answer an email.

Why it works: it removes the obligation, and an obligation removed is exactly when people answer. Expect roughly a third of the replies you get across the whole sequence to come from this message.
