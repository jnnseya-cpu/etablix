# LinkedIn — Neil Sheridan, Head of Procurement, Balfour Beatty

Of the five, this is the one where being direct is correct. Jonathan is client
side and had to be kept at arm's length; James self-delivers and would have
declined a pitch; Neil's function exists to find and place capable suppliers.
Asking him about the route into a supply chain is not an imposition, it is his
job description.

Three things decide whether he replies:

**Be narrow.** Everyone who writes to a Head of Procurement at a tier one says
they can do everything. Naming four packages and stopping is the single
strongest signal in the message.

**Be honest about being new, in the first half.** He will check, and it takes
him ninety seconds. A new company that says so and proposes a proportionate
first step is a manageable proposition; a new company that lets him discover
it is a closed file.

**Ask for the route, not a favour.** He can answer "here is the onboarding
process and here is what we need to see" without owing anybody anything.

Your credibility with him is unusually specific: you were his counterpart. You
bought civils and site infrastructure into a turnkey scope at an OEM. He will
recognise that immediately, and it is worth more than any adjective.

> Thanks for connecting, Neil.

>

> means I have spent years on your side of the table, trying to place packages
> nobody quite owned.
>

>

> those things are usually five or six orders with five or six interfaces
> between them, and the gaps between them belong to nobody until they cost
> somebody. One order, one accountable party, one throat to hold.
>
> Being straight with you: the company is new. The delivery record is mine

> small and discrete and be judged on it than be admitted broadly and disappoint

> What is the right route to be considered for the Balfour Beatty supply chain,
> and what would you need to see from a company at our stage?
>

## When he answers

**If he points you at an onboarding process or a portal** — do it immediately
and completely. The speed and completeness of that return is the first real
data he has about how you perform, and he is a procurement professional: he is
reading it as a performance sample whether or not he says so.

Two standing rules:

- **Never pay to register on a supplier portal.** A fee to be listed is not a
  route to work, and paying it marks you as a company that does not know that.
- **Declare only accreditations and insurances you hold today.** "In progress,
  expected [month]" is a perfectly respectable answer to a prequalification
  question. A false one ends the relationship, and in his job verifying it is
  routine.

**If he names a package he struggles to place** — that is the most valuable
sentence you will get this month. Ask one follow-up about why (capability,
capacity, compliance, geography), then say honestly whether you can take it.
Declining one and offering the three around it will do more for you than a yes
you cannot deliver.

**If he asks for a capability statement** — send the one at etablix.com, and
nothing else. Do not add a deck.

## What to keep out

- **No claim of a delivery record the company does not have.** The personal
  record is real and strong; borrowing it for the company without saying so is
  the one mistake that would end this permanently.
- **No confidential GE Vernova detail.** Your role and the shape of the scope
  are yours; project values, supplier names and commercial terms are not.
- **No attachment in the first message.** Ask first, send when asked.
