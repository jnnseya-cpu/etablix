# The network introduction — one message, three names

To send to construction contacts on LinkedIn. It asks for an introduction
rather than a meeting, which is the only ask most of them can act on without
needing anybody's permission.

**LinkedIn messages are plain text.** No bold, no bullets, no markdown. The
product names carry themselves in capitals because that is how they are
branded. Line breaks work; nothing else does.

---

## The message

> Hi [first name] — quick note, and no reply needed unless it's useful.
>
> After [n] years running construction subcontracts at GE Vernova — the
> turnkey jobs where the civils, site management and site infrastructure sat
> inside our own contract — I set up ETABLIX to do that one piece properly.
>
> ETABLIX (etablix.com) takes site establishment as one accountable package:
> compound, temporary power and water, welfare, waste, logistics and
> accommodation. One owner, instead of six orders with the gaps between them
> belonging to nobody.
>
> CONSTRUX (construxvg.com) is the operating system we run it on — commercial,
> programme, site, safety, supplier and information control in one system of
> record.
>
> VERYX (veryxjnn.com) is the executive lens across a portfolio — delivery
> risk, performance and decisions in one governed picture.
>
> If that's a problem your business is living with, or somebody in your network
> is, I'd welcome the introduction.
>
> Justin

About 150 words. That is at the top of what a LinkedIn message can carry
before it is scrolled past rather than read.

---

## The short version

For anyone you do not know well, or where the longer one feels heavy:

> Hi [first name] — I've set up ETABLIX (etablix.com), taking site
> establishment as one accountable package: compound, temporary power and
> water, welfare, waste, logistics and accommodation, with one owner instead of
> six orders and gaps between them. It runs on our own platforms, CONSTRUX
> (construxvg.com) for project control and VERYX (veryxjnn.com) for the
> portfolio view.
>
> If that's something your business or anyone in your network is wrestling
> with, I'd welcome an introduction. No reply needed otherwise.
>
> Justin

## Why it is shaped like this

**The first line removes the obligation.** "No reply needed unless it's
useful" reads as confidence rather than pressure, and it raises the reply rate
rather than lowering it. Somebody who feels no duty to answer is free to.

**The second line is the person, not the platform.** People place you as the
man who ran construction subcontracts long before they place a company with
three product names. Lead with the platform and the reader cannot tell in five
seconds whether this is an old colleague, a consultancy or software — and when
somebody cannot categorise a message, they do not answer it. That is the single
most expensive mistake available here, and this line is the guard against it.

**Three names is already the limit.** Each gets one sentence and no more. A
fourth would turn the message into a brochure.

**The ask is an introduction, not a meeting.** A contact who cannot buy from
you can still forward you, and forwarding costs them nothing and makes them
look well-connected. Asking for a meeting asks them to spend something. Asking
for an introduction asks them to spend nothing.

---

## Sending rules

**Personalise the first line every time.** One specific detail — a project,
where you met, something they posted. Without it the message is a broadcast and
reads as one, and construction is a small enough world that two people will
compare notes.

**Send in batches of no more than 15 to 20 a day.** A burst of near-identical
messages to many connections is exactly what LinkedIn's automation detection
looks for, and a restricted account costs far more than a slow campaign. Spread
it over two or three weeks.

**Connections only.** A message to a non-connection lands in "Other" or in
message requests, where a great many people never look. For anybody you are not
connected to, send a connection request with a short note first and the message
after they accept — the day after, not the same minute.

**Log every send in the outreach ledger**, with the date and what came back.
Without that record a run carrying replies will feel like a run carrying none,
which has already happened once.

**Two weeks later, one follow-up at most**, and only where you have something
new to say. Then stop. Nothing about this message is worth chasing.

---

## What not to add

- **No attachment and no deck.** Three links are already the ceiling. A
  document in an opening message reads as a mailshot whatever it contains.
- **No prices, no rates, no fee percentages.** Nothing on LinkedIn publishes a
  price.
- **No claim to a delivery record the company does not have.** The founder's
  record is real and is described as his. The company's is not there yet, and
  anybody who asks should be told so plainly.
- **No statistics.** Not a percentage of projects that overrun, not an industry
  waste figure, nothing. Every number invites a question about its source, and
  a message is the worst place to be asked one.
