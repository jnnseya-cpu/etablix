# The executive cold email

**Use:** a first approach to a project director, commercial director, operations
director or head of procurement at an owner, developer, EPC or major contractor.
No prior contact, no referral, no shared project.

**It closes on the diagnostic.** An earlier draft closed on a question and asked
for nothing. This one asks for the Site Systems Diagnostic, which is a bigger
ask and has to be earned inside the same email — so the last third is built
entirely around removing reasons to say no: documents they already hold, a fixed
fee, a fixed date, a document that is theirs whatever happens next, and a worked
specimen they can judge before committing.

**What it has to survive.** An executive deletes a cold email in under two
seconds on one of three reactions: *who is this*, *what are they selling me*,
and *this could have been sent to anyone*. The opening line is a structural
truth about how the industry prices this scope, the company and its number are
named in the second paragraph, and the ask at the end is nine documents rather
than a meeting.

**Nothing in it can be checked and found false.** No statistics, no client names,
no case studies, no savings figures, no accreditations. Every mechanism it
describes is one the reader can verify against their own cost reports in ten
minutes, which is the point: it does not ask to be believed.

## Why each part is there

**The subject line is the argument, not the offer.** "The one scope that gets
priced before it is specified" is a claim a construction executive either
recognises immediately or disputes immediately, and either reaction opens the
email. An offer in a subject line reads as a sales email and is deleted on sight.

**The opening line does the whole job.** It makes no reference to the reader, so
it cannot be wrong about them, and it describes something they have watched
happen on every project they have run. It is the company's own long-held
position, not a line written for this email — it appears in the scope of service
and in the published blog.

**The second paragraph answers "who is this" before it is asked**, with the
registered number in the first sentence. A procurement reader checks Companies
House; handing them the number unprompted is the cheapest credibility in the
letter.

**"We are not a main contractor and we take no CDM appointment"** kills the
reaction that costs the most. Most recipients hold Principal Contractor and
Principal Designer themselves, and an unknown firm that sounds like it wants
those roles is a competitor. Eleven words convert a competitor into a supplier
line in their plan.

**The three mechanisms are checkable, and nobody is blamed.** Each is verifiable
in the reader's own records without asking anybody, and the sentence that follows
says plainly that none of it is a supplier failure — the structural reason is
that the scope falls due at bid stage when nobody has time. An email implying the
reader's team has been careless is deleted even when it is right.

**"Test us on documents you already hold" is the hinge.** It reframes the
purchase from *buy a consultancy report* to *run a low-cost test on us*, and it
removes the real objection, which is not the fee but the work. They are not being
asked to commission a survey, host a visit or write a brief. They are being asked
to forward a folder that exists.

**The diagnostic is sold on what it does to their own pack**, not on what it
contains. "Reads them against each other" is the whole product: the value is in
finding where the client's own documents disagree, which nobody inside the
project can see because each document is correct in isolation. The twelve
sections are then listed concretely, ending on 30/60/90-day actions, because a
list of named outputs reads as a product and a description of "insight" reads as
consultancy hours.

**Four risk reversals in two sentences.** Fixed scope, fixed fee, one invoice;
the report is theirs whatever happens; nothing follows unless they want it; and
the specimen lets them judge the standard before committing. That last one does
the most work — almost nobody in this market publishes a real worked extract, and
offering one invites exactly the scrutiny a weak firm would avoid.

**The both-ways close.** "If it tells you nothing you did not already know, you
have bought a second opinion in writing" concedes the worst case out loud and
makes it survivable. A reader who has already thought of that objection finds it
answered rather than dodged.

**The ask is nine documents, not a meeting.** It is a smaller commitment than a
diary slot, it is concrete, and saying yes starts the process rather than
scheduling a conversation about starting the process.

## Before sending

**Add one sentence of your own if you have one.** A single specific line about
their project — a scheme they have announced, a site you know, a stage they have
reached — placed immediately after the opening paragraph raises the reply rate
more than anything else here. Send it without one rather than inventing one: a
wrong detail about their own project ends the conversation in a way a generic
email does not.

**Two commitments this email makes on your behalf.** Ten working days from the
last document, and a worked extract published at that address. Both are already
true — the ten days is the standard the engagement is sold on and the clock
starts when the last mandatory item arrives, not when the first does — but do not
soften either in a reply. A turnaround renegotiated after it has been offered is
the first thing a buyer remembers.

**Never attach anything to this.** No capability statement, no CV, no brochure.
A cold email with an attachment reads as a mailshot and is more likely to be
filtered before a human sees it. The one link is to your own domain, which is
normal; a second link is not.

**One name per send.** Do not copy a list. The company number in the signature
is an invitation to check, and a visible distribution list makes checking
pointless.

**What must never be added to this email**, however the conversation goes:

- A price. "Fixed scope, fixed fee" is the limit, and it is already in.
- The Prime Service Contractor model. It is not open for appointment: the
  professional indemnity cover in force insures a consultant and does not
  respond to holding works contracts.
- Any accreditation. There is no ISO certification and no SSIP registration, and
  a claim of either is checkable in one search.
- A reference, a case study or a former employer's project. There is no company
  track record yet, and this email does not pretend otherwise — which is part of
  why it reads as honest.
