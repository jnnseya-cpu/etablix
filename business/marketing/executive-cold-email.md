# The executive cold email

**Use:** a first approach to a project director, commercial director, operations
director or head of procurement at an owner, developer, EPC or major contractor.
No prior contact, no referral, no shared project.

**What it has to survive.** An executive deletes a cold email in under two
seconds on one of three reactions: *who is this*, *what are they trying to sell
me*, and *this could have been sent to anyone*. The email below is built to fail
all three tests quickly — the opening line is a structural truth about how the
industry prices this scope, the company and its number are named in the second
paragraph, and the ask is a question rather than a meeting.

**Nothing in it can be checked and found false.** No statistics, no client
names, no case studies, no savings figures, no accreditations. Every mechanism
it describes is one the reader can verify against their own cost reports in ten
minutes, which is the point: it does not ask to be believed.

## Why each part is there

**The subject line is the argument, not a label.** "The one scope that gets
priced before it is specified" is a claim a construction executive either
recognises immediately or disputes immediately. Either reaction opens the email.
A subject naming the company, the service or a benefit gets neither.

**The opening line does the whole job.** It makes no reference to the reader, so
it cannot be wrong about them, and it describes something they have watched
happen on every project they have run. It is also the company's own long-held
position rather than a line written for this email — it appears in the scope of
service and in the published blog.

**The second paragraph answers "who is this" before it is asked**, with the
registered company number in the first sentence. A procurement reader checks
Companies House. Giving them the number unprompted is the cheapest credibility
in the letter, and a trading name that does not resolve to a registered company
is where a serious reader stops.

**"We are not a main contractor and we take no CDM appointment"** kills the
reaction that costs the most. Most recipients hold Principal Contractor and
Principal Designer themselves, and an unknown firm that sounds like it wants
those roles is competing with them. Saying what we are not, in eleven words,
converts a competitor into a supplier line.

**The three bullets are mechanisms, not claims.** Each one is checkable in the
reader's own records without asking anybody. They are also stated so that no
party is blamed — "it falls due at bid stage when nobody has time" is the
structural reason, and the sentence that follows the bullets says so explicitly.
An email that implies the reader's team has been careless is deleted even when
it is right.

**One engagement route, not a menu.** Advisory only. It is the lowest-risk yes
available — a fixed scope for a fixed fee, with every supplier contract staying
with the reader — and a cold email is the wrong place to make somebody choose a
delivery model. The Management Integrator appointment is not mentioned; it comes
up in the conversation this email is trying to start.

**"You contract every supplier, and we never hold your money"** is the sentence
that separates this from every intermediary who has emailed them this month. It
removes the objection before it forms.

**The ask is a question, not a meeting.** "Who is accountable for the off-hire
dates?" takes two seconds to answer, requires no diary, and on most projects the
honest answer is *nobody* — which is the moment the email stops being a cold
approach and starts being a conversation the reader opened. A meeting request at
this point asks for fifteen times more commitment and converts worse.

## Before sending

**Add one sentence of your own if you have one.** A single specific line about
their project — a scheme they have announced, a site you know, a stage they have
reached — placed immediately after the opening paragraph, raises the reply rate
more than anything else in this document. Send it without one rather than
inventing one: a wrong detail about their own project ends the conversation in a
way a generic email does not.

**Never attach anything to this.** No capability statement, no CV, no brochure.
A cold email with an attachment is read as a mailshot and is more likely to be
filtered before a human sees it.

**One name per send.** Do not copy a list. The company number in the signature
is an invitation to check; a visible distribution list makes checking pointless.

**What must never be added to this email**, however the conversation goes:

- A price. "Fixed scope, fixed fee" is the limit, and it is already in.
- The Prime Service Contractor model. It is not open for appointment: the
  professional indemnity cover in force insures a consultant and does not
  respond to holding works contracts.
- Any accreditation. There is no ISO certification and no SSIP registration, and
  a claim of either is checkable in one search.
- A reference, a case study or a former employer's project. There is no company
  track record yet and this email does not pretend otherwise — which is part of
  why it reads as honest.
