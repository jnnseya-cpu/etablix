# GE Vernova — why the three went quiet, and stopping

Martin Shaw, Mark Stone and Marc Townsend, plus others on LinkedIn. Two rounds
have gone: a first email that read as a supplier introduction, and the
follow-up sequence in `followup-week-two.html` written to correct it.

**My last message said no GE Vernova approach existed in this repository. That
was wrong — I searched only the markdown files. The sequence is in the HTML
campaign files and I wrote it.**

## The defect is in the strategy, and it is mine

Look at what happened either side of the line.

**Strangers confirmed the diagnosis.** Jason Jackson at Siemens Energy
confirmed it unprompted and then improved it. Gary Coleman at Hitachi Energy
described the same fragmentation without being asked and offered a referral.

**Insiders could not.** Martin, Mark and Marc were sent the same diagnosis and
said nothing.

Same proposition, same quality of writing, opposite results. The variable is
not the message. It is what agreeing with it costs the reader.

The whole sequence rests on one observation: that an equipment and EPC business
is not a site-establishment business, so that scope gets built from nothing on
every job. For a stranger, that is an interesting industry observation they can
discuss freely. For Martin, it is a statement that his engineers' hours are
being wasted. For Marc, that sourcing repeats work every cycle. For Mark, that
the business he directs has a standing capability gap.

**Confirming it in writing, to a former colleague who now sells the remedy, is
something none of them can do.** Not because they dislike him. Because it is a
written admission about their own department, sent to an external party with a
commercial interest in it. The safest available action is silence, and all
three of them independently reach it.

That is the explanation. It is structural, it is complete, and it has nothing
to do with how he left.

### Two smaller faults in the same drafts

**The sequence says "0 pitch" and then pitches.** The Martin email describes
the survey, the site report, the output specification, the package boundaries,
the interface matrix and the tender basis for a fixed fee. The Marc email sets
out two commercial steps. That is a better-written supplier introduction, which
is precisely the thing the second round existed to stop being.

**Three people in one business.** The page warns that they talk to each other,
then sends to all three anyway with spacing as the mitigation. Spacing does not
remove it. Once one of them is unsure how to answer, nobody wants to be the one
who answered.

## Stop. The sequence itself says where.

From `followup-week-two.html`, written before any of this:

> Then one line each, three weeks later, and stop. With people you used to work
> with, a fourth email costs you the relationship you are actually trying to
> keep.

That point has been reached. **Do not send the specimen diagnostic.** A fourth
contact carrying a report converts a quiet non-event into a memorable
imposition, and in a large listed company with a formal compliance function
there is a real chance that a persistent approach from a former employee is
forwarded to somebody whose job is to log it. That would create the record he
is afraid already exists — and persistence, not the original approach, is what
creates it.

If the closing line has not gone, send it once and mean it:

> No reply needed — I know what your inboxes look like. If the site
> infrastructure scope ever becomes somebody's problem again, you know where I
> am.

Then leave it entirely.

## Check the contract before anything else

Read the GE Vernova employment contract for non-solicitation and confidentiality
clauses. These commonly run six to twelve months from the leaving date.

If a restricted period is still running, it explains the silence completely and
it gives a date rather than an anxiety. They may have been reminded of it. It
also means the same three people become free to talk when it expires, which
makes waiting a strategy rather than a defeat.

## The route that needs nobody to reply

**Supplier registration.** Every tier-one original equipment manufacturer buys
through supply chain management, not through a construction director's inbox.
Gary Coleman said exactly this about Hitachi Energy: to become part of it, you
register with the supply chain.

GE Vernova is no different. If they ever buy this scope, they buy it that way.
Registration is impersonal, it raises no conflict question, it needs no
individual to take a risk on replying, and it survives every personal
relationship being cold.

Two standing rules apply: never pay to register, and list only accreditations
genuinely held.

## The 225-bed set is clean — checked

The postscript in the Marc email offers an Employer's Requirements set for a
225-bed workforce village. That is ETABLIX's own specimen, built in this
repository from its own generator scripts, on a fictional project. Its room
schedule is 225 = 199 + 12 + 14, asserted by the audit script that builds it.

Skye was **309 beds over 36 months**. Different project, different figures,
nothing carried across. The document is his to show, and there is no
confidentiality question in it.

## The timing is the missing piece, and it changes the reading

He left GE Vernova in **February 2026**, with a six-month period during which he
could not contact them. That period ran to roughly **August 2026**. Today is
mid-September, so it expired about a month ago.

**Which branch applies decides what happened.**

**If the first email went out before August**, it landed inside the restricted
period. The silence is then fully explained and is not a judgement: a message
from a former employee inside that window puts the recipient in a position
where replying could implicate them, and the safe action is to do nothing and
mention it to nobody.

**If he waited and only made contact after August**, nothing was breached. But
the recipients do not diary the expiry of somebody else's restriction. They
remember there was one. A message from a former colleague they last knew was
restricted still produces "check before answering" rather than "reply", and
checking is how a message dies.

Either branch gives the same instruction: stop, and let time do the work.

**And February is only seven months ago.** That is very recent. Those three are
still working alongside whoever absorbed his work. In their minds the departure
is current, not historic. The same approach a year from now, carrying a
delivery record, is a completely different object — and the restriction being
so recent is itself the reason none of them can respond naturally yet.

Read the actual clause rather than relying on the six-month figure from memory.
These clauses often cover more than contact, they can run from a date other than
the last working day, and what they restrict is usually solicitation rather than
conversation. This is not legal advice and the contract is the authority.

## The fact worth more than any of this

**309 beds. 36 months.**

That is a materially stronger credential than anything in the outreach drafts,
and it is going in the wrong place. A 36-month EPC project carrying a 309-bed
workforce village inside its scope is exactly the substantial, specific,
checkable reference the DPS applications need and do not currently have.

It belongs in the curriculum vitae and in the question 6.1 explanation, written
as scale and role — the duration, the bed count, the scope that sat inside the
contract, the interfaces personally managed — and not as the client's
commercial terms. That distinction is ordinary CV practice and it keeps the
line between what is his to describe and what is not.

## Where the hours are worth more

Two live conversations are open and unworked: the Hitachi Energy referral Gary
Coleman offered, and the Jason Jackson thread. Both are at tier-one OEMs, both
are warm, and neither carries any of the problems above.

Nobody at GE Vernova can buy from him this year without an internal argument
somebody has to volunteer to have. That is not a market. It is a hope.
