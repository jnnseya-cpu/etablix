# Reply — Flamecore Modular Building Solutions (China)

**From:** Sherwin Yin, Flamecore, Beijing
**Via:** the subcontractor/supplier invitation
**Asked:** do we evaluate China-based supply partners for site offices, welfare
and worker accommodation; and what are our technical prequalification
requirements, preferred module specifications and relevant destination
approvals
**Attached:** an eight-page capability profile
**Date:** 14 October 2026

## What the profile actually shows

Six manufacturing locations across five provinces, a 200,000 sqm production
footprint and 1.3 million sqm of designed annual capacity. Fifty-eight listed
export projects across twenty-eight countries, 2018 to 2026. Workforce camps
are in the record — a Zhejiang camp of 1,200 units delivered in 45 days — along
with a 1,800-module Beijing apartment hotel, a 138-room modular hotel at 90 per
cent factory completion, a Beijing school and the Hong Kong emergency hospital.
The named overseas case study is South Queensland: 23 two-storey townhouses,
60 days from production start to dispatch-ready, with design, manufacture and
full factory fit-out in their scope.

**Two things in that document decide the reply.**

**Their European record is Germany and Montenegro. There is no United
Kingdom.** So the question is not whether they can build a module — the camp
and hotel numbers answer that — but whether they have ever taken one through
Great Britain's approvals and contracting route. Nothing in eight pages says
they have.

**The certification page carries its own asterisk:** *"Certifications held by
selected manufacturing partners."* That is honest, and it is also precisely
what a prequalification will open first. Flamecore presents as an integrated
network rather than a single factory, which means the ISO certificates, the
factory production control certificate and the audit history all belong to a
specific plant that has to be named per enquiry.

**The Australia project is the encouraging part and should be said so.**
Australia also required destination approvals, certification a local authority
would accept, and a contracting route a local client could enforce. They got
through it once. That is a far better predictor than the capacity figures.

> **1. Technical prequalification — what a Great Britain construction site
> actually requires.** These are the items that stop overseas modules at the
> gate, in the order they usually bite:
>
> - **Structural steelwork.** A Declaration of Performance to BS EN 1090-1 from
>   a named factory with certified factory production control, the Execution
>   Class stated, and the product marked as currently accepted for
>   construction products placed on the market in Great Britain. The marking
>   regime has moved more than once since 2021, so confirm the position at the
>   time of enquiry rather than relying on a 2023 note.
> - **Design loading.** Eurocodes with the **UK National Annexes** — in
>   particular snow to BS EN 1991-1-3 and wind to BS EN 1991-1-4. A module
>   designed to another country's loading map is the most common structural
>   rejection, and it is not a paperwork problem: it changes the frame.
> - **Fire.** Reaction-to-fire classification to BS EN 13501-1 for linings and
>   external surfaces, with test reports from a laboratory whose accreditation
>   a British building control body and a British insurer will both accept.
>   Separately, site accommodation is normally caught by the insurers' Joint
>   Code of Practice on fire prevention on construction sites, which drives
>   separation distances between units, limits on combustible insulation and
>   alarm provision. Sleeping accommodation also needs detection and alarm to
>   BS 5839 and a means-of-escape strategy for the block as arranged on site,
>   not for a single unit in isolation.
> - **Electrical.** The quiet killer. Units must be wired to BS 7671, the IET
>   Wiring Regulations — 230 V at 50 Hz, British socket outlets, residual
>   current protection, and an installation certificate from a competent person
>   in this country. A unit that arrives wired to another standard is rewired
>   here, and the rewire usually consumes the price advantage that justified
>   the shipping.
> - **Welfare content.** For a construction site the unit has to deliver what
>   Schedule 2 of the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015
>   requires — sanitary conveniences, washing facilities, drinking water,
>   changing rooms and lockers, and rest facilities — in the numbers the
>   workforce served demands. That is a quantity question before it is a
>   product question.
> - **Lifting and transport.** Certified lifting points with current test
>   certificates, and module dimensions checked against British road limits.
>   Above those limits it becomes an abnormal load, with notification periods
>   and escorts that change both programme and cost.
> - **Thermal performance.** Clients increasingly set U-values for temporary
>   accommodation whether or not the regulations compel it, particularly where
>   the units will run for years rather than months.

## Why it is written this way

**He asked three technical questions, so he gets three technical answers.**
A short, warm, content-free reply would have been easier and useless. He asked
what the prequalification requirements are; telling him properly costs an hour
and is the only thing in the exchange he cannot get elsewhere.

**Answering "is it relevant" directly, in the first line.** He offered to stop
following up if it was not. Leaving that unanswered while taking his
information would be the rude option, not the polite one.

**Origin is addressed and dismissed as a factor in one sentence.** He asked
whether China-based partners are evaluated, which means he has been refused for
that reason before. The honest answer is that the factory's location is not the
problem and the approvals and the contracting route are, and saying so plainly
is more useful than diplomatic vagueness.

**The electrical paragraph is the most valuable thing in the letter.** BS 7671
is where overseas units actually fail, and the reason is commercial rather than
technical: a rewire on arrival eats the margin that justified the freight. Most
people would discover that after the first container.

**The commercial gate is given its own section because it outranks the
technical one.** A perfect module from an unsuable counterparty does not get
bought. Naming the three routes that do work — local entity, local distributor
holding the contract, or parent guarantee with English jurisdiction and
security — is advice he can act on this week.

**The labour due diligence paragraph is stated as procedure, not suspicion.**
It is a real and standard requirement of British public bodies and tier-one
contractors, he will meet it at every door, and a supplier who has the audits
ready is genuinely advantaged. Framing it as a concern about his business would
have been both offensive and wrong; omitting it would have been a disservice.

**Their own asterisk is read back to them.** The profile says the ISO
certificates belong to selected manufacturing partners. That is honest of them
and it is also the first thread an assessor pulls, so the advice is to name the
plant per enquiry instead of presenting the network.

**The Australia point is the generous note and it is also the true one.** Their
strongest argument to a British buyer is not 1.3 million square metres of
capacity; it is that they have already taken a module through another
jurisdiction's approvals and come out the other side. Telling a supplier which
of their own credentials is the strong one is the most memorable thing in any
reply to a cold introduction.

**The domain-address note is said once, practically, and without
condescension.** A gmail address and a WhatsApp number will cost him
credibility at every British assessment desk. He should hear it from somebody
who replied rather than infer it from everybody who did not.

## What the reply withholds

No price, and no suggestion of volume, value or a pipeline — the no-live-enquiry
sentence is in the second paragraph precisely so nothing that follows reads as
an opportunity. No accreditation is claimed for ETABLIX, which holds none. No
client, scheme or former employer is named. Nothing implies a Principal
Designer or Principal Contractor appointment: every regulatory item is
described as a requirement the product must meet, not a duty ETABLIX holds. And
no regulatory claim is stated more confidently than it deserves — the
construction-products marking regime has changed repeatedly since 2021, so the
letter says to confirm the current position at enquiry rather than asserting
one.
