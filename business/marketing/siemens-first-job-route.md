# Turning Jason's reply into a first engagement

## The rule that decides this

**His words never leave the conversation he said them in.** He wrote that in a
private message, unprompted, to a peer from his own industry, being helpful. If
it turns up in front of anyone at Siemens Energy as "your Programme Director
told me you have a problem", three things happen: he stops replying, the person
you quoted it to closes ranks, and the story reaches other people in a sector
where everyone knows everyone. It would be the single most expensive sentence
in your first year.

It also would not work. Nobody buys because a colleague was quoted at them.

**But asking Jason himself is entirely legitimate**, and it is the natural end
of the conversation he has chosen to have. He is not a bystander you are
leveraging — he is the person who confirmed the problem and sharpened the
diagnosis over two messages. Asking whether there is something small worth
testing it on is the obvious next question between two people who have got
that far.

---

## The sequence

Do not compress it. Each step earns the next.

**Now — reply to what he said.** Concede the interface point, add the power
example from the buying side, ask the one question about what actually settles
interface ownership: a document, or people on site. That message is already
written and it contains no ask.

**When he answers that — the ask.** Two things, framed so either can be
declined without awkwardness. If he does not answer the interface question,
leave it: one good reply from a programme director is not permission to ask
for work, and the ask keeps.

**In parallel, and starting today — supplier registration.** This is the real
gating item and it has nothing to do with Jason. A company the size of Siemens
Energy cannot raise a purchase order to a supplier who is not on their system,
and onboarding takes weeks: insurances, accreditations, financials, policies,
bank verification. Start it now, so that when there is a reason to need it, it
is done. Ask Jason who handles it — that is a separate, small, easy question,
and he can answer it without committing to anything.

---

## The ask, when the moment comes

Not now. This goes after he answers the interface question — his second reply,
not his first. He has given you one considered answer; coming back with a
request for work immediately teaches him that answering costs him something.

> Jason — that was more useful than a month of my own guessing. Two things, and
> you can say no to either without it being awkward.
>
> First: would you let me test it on something real? Not a proposal and not a
> sales process. One site or one package, your choice. I take whatever
> information pack exists and produce two things — the interface matrix, saying
> who owns each boundary, and a reconciliation of what your own documents
> actually say against each other. Ten working days from the day the pack lands.
>
> No fee for the first one. What I want out of it is your critique, and — only
> if it turns out to be good — your agreement that I can say it was done and
> that it was useful. If it is not good, you have lost nothing but the pack you
> sent me, and I would rather find that out on something small.
>
> Second, and separately: whoever the right answer to that is, who inside
> Siemens Energy handles supplier registration? I would rather be through that
> process before there is a reason to need it than afterwards.
>
> No rush on either.

---

## Why no fee on the first one, and what it costs you

It is the wrong answer in general and the right answer here, and it is worth
being clear-eyed about why.

**What it buys.** No fee means no purchase order, which means no procurement
process, no supplier onboarding gate, and no budget-holder conversation — the
three things that stop a new company getting inside a large one. It reduces
the decision to one person saying "go on then", which is the only decision
Jason can make on his own.

**What it costs.** Free work is valued at what it cost. You mitigate that by
being precise about scope and time — two named deliverables, ten working days,
one site — so it reads as a defined pilot rather than an open offer. Never
extend it, never do "a bit more while I am in there", and never repeat it for
the same client. The second piece of work is paid or it does not happen.

**What you actually want from it.** Not the money. A reference, a real pack of
documents to prove the method on, and a sponsor inside a tier-one energy
business. Those three are worth more than any first fee you could have charged,
and you cannot buy them.

**One thing to watch.** Some organisations cannot accept unpaid work from a
prospective supplier — it cuts across their own procurement and conflict rules.
If Jason says so, that is not a rejection: ask what the smallest engagement is
that their process can accommodate, and take that instead.

---

## If he says yes

**Ask for the pack, not for a meeting.** The whole proposition is that you work
from their documents. Send the requirement list, take what arrives, and do not
chase what does not.

**Deliver early and to the day.** Ten working days means ten working days.
Arriving on day nine is as much a broken promise as day eleven if you told him
ten, so tell him the date and hit it.

**Put the interface matrix first.** It is the thing he named, it is the thing
his team cannot produce at bid stage, and it is what he will judge the whole
report on. Everything else in the twelve deliverables is supporting evidence.

**Say what you could not determine.** Where the pack did not support a
conclusion, write that down and say what it prevented. A shorter honest report
beats a longer invented one, and with an engineer of his standing the invented
parts are the only parts he will remember.

## If he says no

It is not a loss. Two exchanges with a programme director who has told you what
actually goes wrong is the most valuable thing that has happened to this
business, and it cost nothing. Thank him, ask the supplier-registration
question anyway, and keep him informed occasionally — not with a pitch, but with
what you learned. The person who tells you the truth once will usually do it
again if you do not make them regret it.

## The separate permission ask, much later

When you have something worth showing — a delivered engagement, a page of the
website that describes the interface problem properly — go back and ask, as its
own message:

> That line of yours about interface ownership has ended up being how I
> describe the whole problem. Are you comfortable with me using that framing
> publicly? Not attributed to you or to Siemens unless you would rather it was,
> and I am equally happy to keep it unattributed.

Asking that is what makes it his to give. Using it without asking is what makes
it something taken.
