# "Main barriers to scaling in UK infrastructure" — 200 words

This is the field where the position is strongest, because the honest answer
is genuinely useful to the programme. A programme built to help SMEs scale into
infrastructure wants barriers it can act on, not a complaint.

Each one below is specific, true of this sector rather than of business
generally, and named so that somebody could do something about it.

---

## The answer — 200 words

> Five, in the order they bite.
>
> **Evidence gates calibrated to incumbents.** Selection stages ask for three
> comparable contracts, and for accreditations — SSIP, ISO 9001, 14001, 45001,
> Constructionline — that presume a workforce to audit and a trading history to
> sample. A capable new entrant fails before anybody reads what it does.
>
> **Financial standing applied mechanically.** Turnover thresholds and credit
> scores are scored by rule rather than judgement. A company in its first years
> fails regardless of competence, and the only remedy usually recognised is a
> guarantee.
>
> **The split between who feels the problem and who can buy.** Project and
> construction directors live with unowned interfaces daily but cannot engage a
> supplier. Supply chain management can engage, does not feel it, and is
> reachable only through a portal. The proposition rarely reaches anybody
> holding both.
>
> **Data access, which is circular.** We cannot demonstrate without a client's
> pre-construction information, and nobody releases that to an unproven
> supplier without a relationship that itself requires a demonstration.
>
> **Cycle length against cash.** Dynamic purchasing system admission and
> mini-competition run in months. A pre-revenue company must outlast a
> procurement cycle it cannot influence — which is a funding problem disguised
> as a sales problem.

---

## Why these five

**They are sector-specific, not generic.** "Access to finance" and "brand
awareness" are true of every small business and tell an assessor nothing. Three
comparable contracts, SSIP, Constructionline and Achilles are the actual gates
in this industry, and naming them correctly proves you have stood at them.

**The circular data barrier is the one that justifies the budget.** It explains
in one sentence why £1,200 of the £8,000 goes on data agreements and a secure
workspace, and why the demonstration is designed as a retrospective run on the
client's own pack. The barrier, the demonstration design and the budget line
all point at each other, which is what makes an application read as thought
through rather than assembled.

**The last line reframes the whole answer.** A funding problem disguised as a
sales problem is precisely what a support programme can address, and saying so
tells the assessor what you would actually use them for.

**Nothing here blames the buyers.** Every barrier is described as a structural
consequence rather than as somebody's failure. Assessors work with these
organisations; an applicant who sounds aggrieved about procurement sounds like
an applicant who will be difficult to introduce.

## Before you paste it

**Bold headings may be stripped by the form.** It still reads correctly as
plain paragraphs, but check — five bold phrases becoming ordinary text can make
the section look like one block.

**If the form's examples list differs from these**, re-order to lead with
whichever of their examples you genuinely face. The five are ordered by how
hard they bite, but matching the assessor's own framing is worth more than the
ordering.

**Do not add a sixth.** Five specific barriers read as analysis; seven reads as
a list of excuses, and the marginal ones dilute the strong ones.
