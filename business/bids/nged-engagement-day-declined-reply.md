## Why it is this short, and why it concedes the point

**It does not argue.** She made a capacity decision and gave the real criterion:
capabilities most closely aligned with the day's objectives. Litigating that
spends the goodwill her letter deliberately left intact — she wrote three
sentences saying the door is open, which is more than a form rejection needed to
say.

**Conceding is the strongest available move, because it is also true.** The
engagement day is for the firms who will hold the licence-area contracts for
underground cabling and overhead wood pole work. ETABLIX is a preliminaries line
inside those firms' delivery, not a competitor for the partnership. Saying so
before she has to tells her we understand her market structure, and it converts
us from an unsuccessful applicant into somebody whose read of the programme is
worth a line of reply.

**The ask is the only thing in the letter that asks for anything, and it costs
her nothing.** "Is there a route to the appointed partners?" is answerable in one
sentence, needs no approval, and is the question that actually matters: the
buying decision for site establishment will be made by the partners, not by NGED
procurement. Her letter offered "the appropriate procurement channels in due
course", which is the portal. The partners are the better door and she is the
cheapest person to ask about it.

**One technical point, given away free.** Four licence areas across two
reinstatement specifications is a category buyer's problem, not a contractor's,
and it lands in the rate schedule. It is the one fact in the letter she can check
in five minutes and the reason the letter gets filed rather than deleted.

## What was checked before it went in

**Verified this week.** The England fourth edition applies in England only; Wales
works to its second edition, issued under SI 2006/72; both sit under s.71 of the
New Roads and Street Works Act 1991. Sources are consistent on the England/Wales
split and on the fourth edition being current for England.

**Deliberately left out.** The earlier draft of the capability covering letter
said the England fourth edition was "statutory from 10 May 2021" and cited an "EV
cable addendum of 30 May 2025". The first is secondary-sourced — the GOV.UK
document itself carries 2018 and 2020 dates — and the second could not be
re-verified. Neither is needed here, and one unconfirmable date in front of a
category buyer undoes the three facts around it. **If the capability statement is
still to be sent, both need checking first.** Owner: Justin Nseya.

## Two defects in the email already sent, which this one corrects

Nothing sent is rewritten. The next letter corrects them by being right.

**The signature named no legal entity and no company number.** It read "ETABLIX —
Integrated Site Services · Part of Groupe Nseya", and the confidentiality footer
said "ETABLIX is part of the Groupe Nseya structure". Both assert a parent entity
that does not exist, and the standing rule is explicit that the company number
belongs in the signature block of any approach to a procurement or supply-chain
reader — which a Senior Category Buyer holding MCIPS is precisely. It is the
first thing they need and the cheapest line in the letter, and it was missing
from a letter to the one reader who checks.

**CONSTRUX and VERYX were in the signature.** Two software products under a
site-services capability pitch. To a buyer deciding whether this firm is focused
enough to put on a list for temporary works, they read as a firm selling three
unrelated things.
