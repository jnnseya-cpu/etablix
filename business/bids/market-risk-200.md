# "Market potential and key risks" — 200 words

For the field asking about market size, competitive landscape, adoption
barriers and mitigation.

**It cites a real, current, checkable source and then refuses to misuse it.**
NISTA's UK infrastructure pipeline carries 734 projects worth £718 billion.
That figure is the market's *context*, not the addressable market, and the
answer says so in the same breath. Applicants who quote a national pipeline
figure as though it were their own addressable market are the ones assessors
discount fastest.

**Note the body, not the old one.** NISTA — the National Infrastructure and
Service Transformation Authority — was formed on 1 April 2025 from the merger
of the National Infrastructure Commission and the Infrastructure and Projects
Authority. Writing "IPA" in 2026 tells an infrastructure assessor you last
looked at this market two years ago.

## The answer — 199 words

> **Market.** NISTA's UK infrastructure pipeline lists 734 projects worth £718
> billion over ten years and now publishes workforce demand alongside it. That
> workforce data is the leading indicator for our scope: headcount determines
> welfare and accommodation. Our addressable slice is the site establishment
> scope inside projects carrying it — energy, nuclear, transport, utilities —
> not a share of that headline. We size bottom-up per engagement.
>
> **Competition.** Common data environments hold documents. Consultancies sell
> people. Neither owns the interfaces between packages. CONSTRUX does not try to
> displace an incumbent platform — ports and adapters with a public API mean it
> interoperates — and VERYX sits above whatever is already there.
>
> **Risks and mitigation.**
>
> No corporate delivery record — the largest barrier. Mitigated by routes where
> selection weighs individual competence rather than company history, principally
> public sector dynamic purchasing systems, and by leading with a fixed-fee
> advisory engagement small enough to buy without a governance argument.
>
> Key-person concentration, one director. Mitigated by method documented in
> CONSTRUX and staged hiring against revenue.
>
> Procurement inertia. Mitigated through supplier registration and DPS
> admission, which require nobody's goodwill.
>
> Cash exposure. Under our management model contracts sit in the client's name;
> we never hold supply-chain money.

## Why each part earns its place

**The workforce-demand sentence is the one that shows you know this market.**
NISTA added workforce demand data to the pipeline, and headcount is precisely
what sizes welfare and accommodation. Very few applicants will connect those
two, and an infrastructure assessor will recognise the connection immediately.
It is one sentence and it does more than a paragraph of market description.

**"Not a share of that headline" is a credibility move.** It says you know the
difference between a pipeline value and a serviceable market, which most
applicants demonstrate they do not.

**The risk section names the worst risk first and in plain words.** "No
corporate delivery record" is what the assessor is already thinking. Writing it
before they do converts it from a hidden objection into a managed one, and the
mitigation is specific and verifiable rather than aspirational — the DPS route
genuinely does weigh individual competence, and the fixed-fee advisory
engagement genuinely is small enough to buy without a governance case.

**The cash mitigation is structural, not promised.** Contracts placed in the
client's name under the management model is how the business is built, not a
concession offered under questioning. That distinction is worth stating because
assessors have read a great many promises.

## Before you paste it

**Verify the pipeline figures on NISTA's own portal.** They were 734 projects
and £718 billion at the last update I could see, but the pipeline is refreshed
and a stale number in a market section is the error an infrastructure assessor
is most likely to catch. If the figures have moved, use the current ones.

**Bold headings may not survive the form.** If it strips formatting, the answer
still reads correctly as plain paragraphs — but check, because five bold words
becoming five ordinary ones can push a paragraph over a line and make the
section look unstructured.

**Do not add a growth projection.** A revenue forecast in a 200-word market
section is unevidenced by definition and invites the one question you cannot
answer yet. The forecast belongs in the financial section, with its assumptions
stated, where it can be defended.
