# Pricing review — every model, every deliverable

**Prepared 9 September 2026. ADOPTED — the catalogue now carries these figures.**

The Managing Director's instruction was to take numbers that reflect the level
of quality produced and price accordingly. Every recommendation below is now in
the system as a named band with the scope that makes its fee fixed, and Models B
and C carry the fee structure they had been missing. What remains outstanding is
section 9: none of this has been tested against the market, and four people can
each turn one part of it into evidence with a single question.

Two things before any number. First, every market level in this paper is my
reasoning from construction fee conventions and from the arithmetic of your own
catalogue — it is **not a surveyed market**, and I have not priced a competitor's
proposal. Test two of these positions with someone who buys this work before you
commit: Aaron Hall and Neil Brooks can both tell you in one sentence whether a
number is laughable, and neither is a prospect you would lose by asking. Second,
nothing here is published anywhere. The website carries no price and should not;
a price belongs in a proposal against a defined scope.

---

## 1 · The finding, in one paragraph

**You are materially underpriced on Model A, and Models B and C have no prices at
all.** The five Model A deliverables sit in ranges that were set before the
delivery system existed and before anyone had seen what it produces; the
diagnostic's floor of £2,500 implies a day rate below a graduate's. Models B and
C — the two that carry the real revenue — have `low: null, high: null` in the
catalogue and no fee basis written down anywhere, so the first time you win one
you will invent the number under pressure, in front of a client, against a
procurement person who does this every week. The recommendation is to raise Model
A, replace ranges with bands, and write a fee structure for B and C now while
nothing is at stake.

## 2 · What the current prices actually say

These are the catalogue figures as the system holds them today.

| Deliverable | Model | Current range |
|---|---|---|
| Site-services feasibility review / Site Systems Diagnostic | A | £2,500 – £7,500 |
| Site Management Requirements Package | A | £7,500 – £25,000 |
| Workforce Village Requirements Package | A | £10,000 – £35,000 |
| Procurement management and tender evaluation | A | £7,500 – £30,000 |
| Mobilisation-readiness / village-readiness review | A | £5,000 – £15,000 |
| Management Integrator appointment | B | **none** |
| Prime Service Contractor appointment | C | **none** |

**The arithmetic of the diagnostic.** It is sold as ten working days from
information handover. At the top of the range that is £750 a day. At the bottom
it is £250 a day. For reference, in UK construction consultancy a graduate sits
around £250–£400, a senior consultant or project manager £600–£900, and an
associate or director £900–£1,500. Your floor prices a director's judgement at a
graduate's rate.

The ten days is elapsed time, not effort — the agent carries the reading and the
first draft, and your real input is two to three days of review, correction and
presentation. So the top of the range is defensible on effort. **The floor is
not defensible on anything.**

**And effort is the wrong argument anyway.** The NORTHREACH report found a £3.1m
generation tender in evaluation against a load the client had themselves
corrected in writing — a 19.6% error on connected load, which is a six-figure
exposure on its own. It found an access milestone 43 to 71 days undeliverable
against a fixed outage window. It found 211 beds required against about 90 rooms.
**Charging £2,500 to find £600,000 is not a bargain; it is a credibility
problem.** A commercial director who sees a forty-page report at £2,500 concludes
one of three things: it is a template, it is a loss-leader with a hook attached,
or the supplier does not understand what they have found. All three cost you the
work.

## 3 · The three mechanics that should set the price

**Delegated authority, not value.** Every main contractor runs an authority
ladder. The bands vary, but the shape does not: below roughly £5,000 a project
manager signs alone; from there to roughly £25,000 a commercial manager or
project director signs; above that you are into three quotes, a procurement
process and a supplier onboarding gate that takes weeks. **A price just under
the second threshold is worth more to you than a lower one**, because it converts
a procurement exercise into one person saying yes. That is the single most
useful thing about pricing a new company's entry product, and £2,500 wastes it —
you get the easy signature and give away two-thirds of the fee to do it.

**A range is a negotiation you have already lost.** "£2,500–£7,500" printed
anywhere a client can see it means £2,500. Nobody has ever read a range and
chosen the top. Bands fix this: three named scopes, three fixed prices, and the
conversation becomes which band this project is, not what the number should be.

**Fixed fee is a promise about scope, not about effort.** Every band below states
what it covers. Without that, a fixed fee on an unbounded document set is an
open-ended commitment, and the first client with eighteen documents and five
compounds teaches you that lesson at your own expense.

## 4 · Model A — deliverable by deliverable

### 4.1 Site Systems Diagnostic — **raise, and band it**

| Band | Scope boundary | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| **Band 1** | One site, one compound, up to 10 documents, peak workforce under 200, one revision round | **£6,500** |
| **Band 2** | Two sites or up to 3 compounds, up to 25 documents, peak under 400, one revision round | **£11,500** |
| **Band 3** | Programme or linear scheme, 4+ compounds, 25+ documents, or a temporary estate above £10m | **£18,500** |

*Current: £2,500–£7,500.*

Band 1 keeps you inside a single signature at most contractors, which is the
whole point of an entry product. Band 3 is what NORTHREACH would have been:
five compounds, eighteen documents, 34 months, a £14m–£24m temporary estate.
£18,500 against a £10m spread of unresolved decisions is not a difficult
argument to make — it is the easiest one in this paper.

**What each band must state as its boundary**, or the fee is not fixed: the
number of documents read, the number of compounds, one revision round, ten
working days from complete handover, and that a pack arriving in pieces restarts
the clock from the last piece.

### 4.2 Site Management Requirements Package — **raise**

| Band | Scope | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Band 1 | One compound, up to 6 packages specified | **£14,000** |
| Band 2 | Multi-compound, up to 12 packages | **£26,000** |
| Band 3 | Programme, 12+ packages, or requirements that go into a framework | **£45,000** |

*Current: £7,500–£25,000.*

This produces the documents that go into a tender for the whole temporary
estate. Professional fees for specification and procurement work conventionally
run 1–3% of the value being procured; on a £15m estate, 1% is £150,000. £45,000
is a quarter of the low end of that convention and still nearly double your
current ceiling. That gap is the size of the mistake.

### 4.3 Workforce Village Requirements Package — **raise**

| Band | Scope | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Band 1 | Up to 150 beds, existing stock only | **£18,000** |
| Band 2 | 150–400 beds, or a mixed strategy | **£32,000** |
| Band 3 | 400+ beds, or a purpose-built facility with a consent route | **£55,000** |

*Current: £10,000–£35,000.*

This is the most specialist thing you sell and the one with the fewest people
who can do it. On NORTHREACH the accommodation decision alone moved between
£3.15m and £11.7m, and the client's entire basis was a graduate's desk research
with no rates in it. Price it as the specialism it is.

### 4.4 Procurement management and tender evaluation — **restructure, do not just reprice**

A fixed fee against an unknown number of packages, rounds and clarifications is
how a consultancy loses money quietly. The catalogue already calls this the
**Managed Procurement Desk** in the website mapping; price it as a desk.

| Shape | Recommended |
|---|---|
| Single package, straightforward — enquiry, evaluation, recommendation | **£4,500** |
| Single package, complex — multi-round, or the requirements written first | **£9,500** |
| Desk retainer, up to 4 live packages | **£6,500 / month** |
| Desk retainer, 5–10 live packages | **£11,500 / month** |

*Current: £7,500–£30,000 as a fixed fee.*

Minimum three months on either retainer. A one-month procurement desk is a
mobilisation with no delivery.

### 4.5 Mobilisation-readiness review — **keep, and band it**

| Band | Scope | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Band 1 | One site, readiness against a fixed mobilisation date | **£5,500** |
| Band 2 | Multi-compound | **£9,500** |
| Band 3 | Programme, or a village included | **£15,000** |

*Current: £5,000–£15,000.*

This is the one to leave broadly where it is. It is narrower than the diagnostic,
it sells at a different moment — weeks before mobilising rather than at bid stage
— and having a cheaper, sharper entry product beneath the diagnostic is useful
rather than confusing, provided the scope difference is stated. Band 1 is your
foot in the door where a client will not buy a full diagnostic.

## 5 · Model B — Management Integrator: the missing structure

There is no price in the catalogue and no fee basis written anywhere. The
machinery exists — the engagement record already carries a monthly fee, a
mobilisation fee and a platform fee — and nothing tells you what to put in them.

**Three components, and all three are chargeable.**

| Component | Basis | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| **Mobilisation and planning fee** | One-off, payable in advance with month one | **£18,000** single site · **£32,000** multi-compound · **£55,000** programme |
| **Monthly integration and management fee** | The core. Guide: **4–7% of the annualised supply-chain spend under management**, with a floor | **Floor £14,000 / month.** Below that the appointment does not fund a Site Integration Manager and you are subsidising it |
| **CONSTRUX platform fee** | Per project per month, where CONSTRUX is in scope | **£1,800 – £4,500 / month** by user count and number of sites |

**Why 4–7%, and why it triangulates.** Build it from cost instead and you get to
the same place: a Site Integration Manager on site full-time is roughly
£8,000–£10,000 a month fully loaded, a part-time project manager £4,000–£5,000,
commercial support £2,000–£3,000, plus central overhead and margin. That is
£18,000–£25,000 a month for a modest appointment. On a £15m estate over 34
months, 5% of the spend is about £22,000 a month. Two different methods, one
answer — which is how you know the number is not invented.

**Minimum term six months.** A shorter appointment does not repay the
mobilisation, and a client who wants three months wants an advisory engagement
with a different name on it.

## 6 · Model C — Prime Service Contractor: the missing structure

Also no price, and this is the one where getting it wrong is not a lost margin
but a lost company. Under Model C you hold the supply chain contracts, which
means you carry the working capital, the credit risk and the performance risk.

| Component | Recommended |
|---|---|
| **Management fee on supplier expenditure** | **9–14%** on the first two engagements. **7–10%** once there is a delivery record and real buying power |
| **Mobilisation fee** | As Model B, plus the procurement build-out — **£35,000 – £90,000** by scale |
| **Advance, payable before any supplier order** | Month-one supplier expenditure + mobilisation + month-one management fee + early procurement commitments + the agreed early-risk contingency |

**Why the first ones are dearer, and say so out loud.** A new prime has no
framework rates, no volume leverage and no track record, so the same scope costs
you more to deliver and carries more risk. Pricing the first two at 9–14% and
saying plainly that it reduces at scale is a stronger position than a low number
you later have to raise. Clients understand a learning curve; they do not
forgive a price rise that was not signalled.

**Three rules that are not negotiable.**

- **No supplier order before the advance has cleared.** The system already says
  this and it is right. It is the single control that stops one client's
  insolvency taking you with it.
- **Never take a prime appointment on a scope you have not priced package by
  package.** The NORTHREACH report is the worked example of why: eighteen of
  thirty-two packages had no buyer and the client's own total was three tendered
  numbers multiplied by 2.4.
- **The six prime gates in the Commercial OS are gates, not guidance.** Working
  capital, insurance, contractual protection, a creditworthy client, back-to-back
  terms and board approval. A prime taken without them is the last engagement.

## 7 · Four commercial rules worth more than the numbers

**Never discount the catalogue. Discount the position.** If you need a reason to
close now, offer a named, dated **founding-client position** — the first three
engagements at 25% of catalogue off, stated as such on the proposal, ending on a
written date. That keeps the list price intact for client four and gives client
one a reason to move. A quiet discount does the opposite of both.

**Never discount the diagnostic to win the integrator appointment.** It teaches
the client that the report was padding, and the integrator fee is then negotiated
against a number you have already shown to be soft.

**Fix the deemed-approval hole before you raise anything.** Seventy per cent of
every Model A fee falls due when the client presses Approve. Today, a client who
simply never approves never triggers the invoice. Every proposal needs a clause
that the deliverable is deemed approved a stated number of working days after
issue unless written comments are received — ten is normal — and the system
should raise the balance on that date. **This is worth more to your cash position
than any price rise in this paper**, and it is a genuine hole rather than a
theoretical one.

**Write down when you raise again.** After three delivered and referenced
engagements, or twelve months, whichever comes first. Put the date in the
calendar now, because the reason companies stay underpriced is that nobody ever
picks a moment.

## 9 · What I could not determine, and who can

Everything above is reasoning, not evidence. Four things would turn it into a
priced position, and none of them costs anything:

- **What a main contractor actually pays for an equivalent report.** Ask Aaron
  Hall. He is an operations director you have known for a decade and it is a
  single question he can answer in a sentence.
- **Where the delegated-authority thresholds actually sit** at the contractors
  you are targeting. Ask Neil Sheridan — a head of procurement knows his own
  ladder to the pound, and the answer decides whether Band 1 is £6,500 or £4,800.
- **What a Management Integrator appointment is worth per month in this sector.**
  Neil Brooks ran a portfolio that bought this scope thirty times.
- **What Hitachi Energy's SCM function expects to see on a rate card** if the
  referral turns into a prequalification. Ask when they ask.

Do not ask all four of the same person, and do not ask any of them as a favour —
each is a question a peer answers gladly and a supplier never gets to ask twice.
