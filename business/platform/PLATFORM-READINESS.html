The honest diagnosis

Not "there were some bugs". A description of how it came to be this way.

This was built feature-first and at speed, and every feature was verified on its own, in sequence, with every dependency healthy. That is why the test suite is green and why it told you nothing useful.

Every fault that actually reached you was of the opposite kind. A mail server that never answered held a request open until the browser gave up, so raising an invoice looked like a failure that had not happened. A container recreated mid-work destroyed six passes of reasoning because only the progress dots were being written down. A document sent twice was appended rather than replaced, so the same evidence would have been counted twice. A salutation that two pieces of code both thought they owned put "Dear Dear" on every email a customer received.

None of those are feature defects. They are integration and operating defects, and they are invisible to the kind of testing that was done. That is the gap, and it is a gap in method rather than in effort.

The deeper issue underneath them is that the data store cannot survive the way it is deployed. That is F1 and F2, and until they are closed nothing else on this list is worth doing.

The register

Severity is by consequence to you, not by effort to fix. Fatal means a client would lose data, lose the service, or be given a number that cannot be defended.

Fatal · F1

The database can be destroyed by a routine deploy

What it is. Every change rewrites the whole of db.json with a single non-atomic writeFileSync. There is no signal handling anywhere in the server, so docker stop kills the process instantly — possibly mid-write.

Why it matters. I truncated db.json to simulate a kill during a write. The server refused to boot: SyntaxError: Unterminated string in JSON at position 39890. The site is then down and every engagement, invoice, checklist answer and document record is unreadable. There is no atomic write, no write-ahead copy and no recovery path.

What closing it takes. Write to a temporary file and rename it — rename is atomic on the same filesystem. Handle SIGTERM: stop accepting connections, finish what is in flight, flush, exit. Keep the previous good copy and fall back to it on a parse failure rather than refusing to start.

Fatal · F2

A JSON file is not a database for a business that issues invoices

What it is. One file, rewritten in full on every mutation. No transactions, no schema, no constraints, no migrations, no concurrent-write safety.

Why it matters. 179 KB today, so speed is not the problem — integrity is. Nothing prevents a half-written record, a lost update or a field that silently changes shape between versions. Invoice numbers, VAT treatment and an audit trail are the things a client will one day dispute, and this store cannot prove what it held on a given date.

What closing it takes. Move to PostgreSQL with real migrations. The store module was written to be swapped without touching the routes, so this is contained work — but it is the work, and nothing else on this list matters if the data underneath is not trustworthy.

Fatal · F3

Backups are documented, not proven

What it is. deploy/backup.sh exists and the runbook tells you to install it as a nightly cron. Nothing in the platform checks that it is installed, that it ran, or that the archive can be restored.

Why it matters. Nightly at best means up to twenty-four hours of loss. An untested backup is not a backup, and today the only copy of every client record is one file in one container on one box.

What closing it takes. Prove a restore, on a schedule, automatically: back up, restore into a scratch instance, boot it, assert the row counts, and alert if any step fails. Copy the archive off the box — a backup on the same machine does not survive the failure that destroys the machine.

Major · M1

Nothing shuts down gracefully

What it is. No SIGTERM or SIGINT handler. In-flight HTTP requests and all background work die the moment the container stops.

Why it matters. This is the mechanism behind every diagnostic run you lost. It is also what makes F1 possible. Pass persistence and the deploy deferral I added reduce the damage; they do not close the gap.

What closing it takes. A shutdown sequence: stop the listener, wait for in-flight requests with a timeout, mark any running pipeline as interrupted so it can be resumed, flush the store, then exit.

Major · M2

The deploy has no gate, no rollback and no version

What it is. Auto-deploy pulls, rebuilds and replaces the container. The health check runs after the switch and only prints a warning if it fails.

Why it matters. You cannot tell which build is live, you cannot go back, and a broken build replaces a working one with nothing to stop it. Every diagnosis today needed me to reproduce locally because there was no way to ask the live site what it was running.

What closing it takes. Build, start the new container alongside, health-check it, switch only if it passes, keep the previous image and provide a one-command rollback. Stamp the commit into /api/health.

Major · M3

There is no staging environment

What it is. Every change went straight to the box you were testing on.

Why it matters. This is the single biggest process failure of the day and it is mine. Repeated pushes recreated the container under you and destroyed run after run. No amount of code quality survives deploying to production while someone is using it.

What closing it takes. A staging instance with its own data, and auto-deploy pointed there. Production deploys become deliberate, from a tag, at a time you choose.

Major · M4

The error alarm depends on the channel that is broken

What it is. An uncaught exception calls reportError, which sends an email.

Why it matters. Mail is exactly what has been failing: DKIM revoked, DMARC silent, and for a period an SMTP host that never answered. The alarm for the building is wired to the fuse that blew.

What closing it takes. Alerts must not depend on one channel. Write to a log the platform exposes, and add a second route out — a webhook or SMS — that does not share a failure mode with email.

Major · M5

An uncaught exception does not stop the process

What it is. The handler logs, attempts an alert, and carries on.

Why it matters. After an uncaught exception the process state is undefined. Continuing to serve requests from it can corrupt exactly the data F1 is already threatening.

What closing it takes. Log, alert, then exit non-zero and let the supervisor restart cleanly. Continuing must be a deliberate exception, never the default.

Major · M6

Secrets sit in the data file

What it is. The AI provider key is stored in the settings collection inside db.json, in plain text — and therefore inside every backup archive.

Why it matters. Anyone with a copy of a backup has the key.

What closing it takes. Secrets belong in the environment or a secret store, never in the data file, and never in a backup that gets copied around.

Major · M7

Public endpoints have no rate limiting

What it is. The human check stops naive bots. Nothing limits volume.

Why it matters. One script can fill the enquiry table, the notification feed and the disk.

What closing it takes. Per-IP and global limits on every public POST, with the limit stated in the response.

Significant · S1

Nothing is watching

What it is. No uptime monitoring, no alert on a failed run, no alert on failed mail, no error surface on the live box.

Why it matters. You found the junked enquiries, the hung Confirm-the-start, and the destroyed runs. The platform found none of them. That is the difference between a prototype and something you can commit to.

What closing it takes. Uptime monitoring from outside the box. An error and event log visible in the Control Desk. An alert when a run fails, when mail fails, and when a backup fails.

Significant · S2

Growth is unbounded in places

What it is. Agent runs and automation runs are trimmed. Notifications are not, and the events array on an engagement grows for the life of the engagement inside a file that is rewritten in full on every change.

Why it matters. Slow, quiet, and it makes F1 worse every week.

What closing it takes. Cap or archive every append-only collection, and move the audit trail out of the row it audits.

Significant · S3

Test coverage is feature-shaped, not failure-shaped

What it is. Around 170 assertions across nine files, all green — while four production faults sat underneath them.

Why it matters. Everything was tested in isolation, in sequence, with every dependency healthy. The faults that reached you were the opposite: a dependency that never answered, a process killed mid-work, a document sent twice, a template two callers both thought they owned. Green tests told you nothing about any of it.

What closing it takes. Test the failure modes explicitly: a dependency that hangs, a dependency that errors, a process killed at each step, the same request sent twice, requests arriving together. The three tests I added this afternoon are that shape and they are the pattern to follow.

Significant · S4

Mail authentication is still broken at the DNS

What it is. Both DKIM selectors publish an empty key, which is a revocation. DMARC has no reporting address.

Why it matters. Every enquiry notification fails authentication. Fixed in the application; the DNS is still to change, and nothing checks it continuously.

What closing it takes. Regenerate the DKIM key, add a DMARC reporting address, and run tools/mail-doctor.mjs on a schedule so a silent regression is caught by the platform rather than by a customer.

Significant · S5

Recovery is manual and undocumented

What it is. There is no runbook step for a corrupt store, a failed deploy, a stuck run or a lost invoice number.

Why it matters. When it goes wrong at 07:00 you will be reading source code.

What closing it takes. A recovery runbook with the actual commands, tested by following it.

Moderate · N1

Whole-array writes are unsafe by construction

What it is. Handlers replace checklist, documents and events wholesale after reading them.

Why it matters. I fired six concurrent uploads and all six survived, because those handlers happen to be synchronous. Adding a single await anywhere between the read and the write reintroduces silent lost updates, and nothing would catch it.

What closing it takes. Append and patch at the store level rather than reading and replacing arrays in route code.

Moderate · N2

No per-route body limits or request timeouts

What it is. Upload limits exist; request timeouts do not.

Why it matters. A slow client can hold a connection indefinitely.

What closing it takes. Server-level request timeout and explicit limits per route.

Moderate · N3

No retention or deletion path for client documents

What it is. Client files are kept indefinitely with no delete.

Why it matters. A client asking for their data to be removed cannot be answered today.

What closing it takes. A retention policy and a deletion path, with the deletion recorded.

What production ready would mean

Written as acceptance criteria, so it is a gate rather than an opinion. Nothing here is met today.

  1. The store survives a kill at any moment. A test kills the process at a hundred random points during sustained writes; after every one, the service boots and no committed record is missing.
  2. A restore is proved automatically. A nightly job backs up, restores into a scratch instance, boots it, checks the counts, and raises an alarm if any step fails.
  3. A deploy cannot take the service down. The new build is health-checked before the switch, the old image is kept, rollback is one command, and the live commit is readable from /api/health.
  4. Every external dependency can fail without taking a request with it. Tested by making each one hang, error and disappear in turn, with the service still answering.
  5. Money is provably correct. Invoice numbering cannot collide or skip; no invoice can be raised twice for one event; VAT treatment is asserted per case; the ledger reconciles to the engagement fee to the penny.
  6. Nothing is discovered by a customer first. Uptime, failed runs, failed mail and failed backups all raise an alert, and the alert path does not share a failure mode with what it is watching.
  7. A soak test runs the whole circle repeatedly for twenty-four hours — with restarts, a dead mail server and a slow model injected — and finishes with the data intact.
  8. Someone other than me can operate it from a runbook that has been followed start to finish by someone who did not write it.

How I would do it

Four stages. Each one ends in something you can check, and I would not move to the next until you have.

StageWhat it doesDone when
0 · Stop deploying into your testTurn auto-deploy off, or point it at a staging instance. Nothing reaches the live box while you are using it.You can test for an hour without anything changing underneath you.
1 · Make the data safeAtomic writes, graceful shutdown, keep-last-good-copy, proved restore, secrets out of the data file.The kill test at criterion 1 passes, and a restore has been demonstrated.

An honest note on scale. This is 11,400 lines of backend and a full front end. Stages 1 and 2 are the substantial ones; 3 is where most of the real confidence is bought. I would rather tell you that now than discover it at stage 3.

What I would ask of you

  1. Decide whether this is worth continuing before I write another line. The prototype proves the idea works end to end — the diagnostic reads a real client pack, the portal invoices itself, the documents are genuinely good. Whether the platform is the right thing to harden, or whether the documents and the method are the product and the platform should be bought rather than built, is a commercial judgement and it is yours.
  2. Do not test on the live box again until stage 0 is done. That was the cause of today, and it was mine.
  3. Fix the DKIM key whatever you decide. That one costs nothing and it is losing you enquiries right now.
