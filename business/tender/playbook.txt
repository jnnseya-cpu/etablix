A method document. It states how ETABLIX packages, procures and — where the client decides to — consolidates a workforce accommodation village. It describes no project, names no client and reports no engagement. Where it states a legal position it does so to inform a commercial decision, not to replace advice on one: novation is a three-party contract whose effect turns on the words used and on the facts, and every mechanism in it needs a lawyer to draft it. 
 
Contents 
 
1  The claim, and the two numbers behind it 
1.1  The claim this document makes 
Five packages consolidated into one is usually described afterwards as a rescue. It is presented as an admission that the packaging was wrong, and the people who chose five spend the rest of the job defending the decision. 
That reading is wrong, and it costs money twice — once when a client who should split does not, and again when a client who has split refuses to consolidate because consolidating would look like a mistake. 
THE CLAIM. Letting five packages and then consolidating them under one prime is a strategy, not a failure, and it is available to any client who writes four clauses into the original five contracts. It buys two things that no other route buys together: five competitively tendered prices, and single-point management of the interfaces between them. Tendering one prime from the start buys the second and gives away the first. Running five to the end buys the first and pays for the second in the client's own management time, which is the most expensive way there is to buy it. 
What makes the difference between a strategy and a rescue is not the decision to consolidate. It is whether the day-one contracts made consolidation possible without four separate negotiations from a position of no leverage. Section 7 is the four clauses. Everything else in this document assumes them. 
1.2  Who this is for, and what it is not 
This is a method document for a client, an employer's agent or a construction manager procuring a workforce accommodation village of two hundred beds or more, on a programme long enough that the accommodation runs alongside a live construction site rather than before it. 
It is not a legal document and it is not legal advice. Novation is a three-party contract and its effect turns on the words used, the law of the contract and the facts. Every mechanism in this document is a commercial structure that a lawyer must draft and advise on. Where this document states a legal position it states it so that the commercial decision is taken with the position in view — not so that it is taken without a lawyer. 
1.3  The two numbers that decide it 
Two numbers run through this document and it is worth stating both at the front. 
THE INTERFACE COUNT. Interfaces between packages grow as n(n−1)÷2. Five packages have ten interfaces between them. Ten packages have forty-five. Adding a sixth package to five adds one contract and five interfaces. The management load is not in the contracts; it is in the gaps between them, and the gaps grow faster than the contracts do. 
THE NOTICE COUNT. Under the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 as amended, every construction contract carries its own payment due date, final date for payment, payment notice deadline and pay-less notice deadline. Five contracts are five sets of dates every month. Miss one pay-less notice and the sum applied for becomes the notified sum and is payable in full, whatever it was worth. Five contracts do not make that five times more likely — they make it more likely than that, because the fifth set of dates is administered by whoever has least time left in the month. 
A client who consolidates five contracts into one has not saved a management fee. It has removed fifty-nine of the sixty statutory notice deadlines it faces each year, and nine of the ten interfaces it was standing in the middle of.

4  The packaging decision: one, five, or five-then-one 
4.1  Three routes, and what each one buys 
There are three ways to buy a workforce accommodation village and the choice is usually made on instinct. It should be made on what each route actually buys. 
ONE PRIME FROM THE START. One tender, one contract, one point of accountability, and the interfaces are the prime's problem from day one. What it buys is management. What it gives away is price: the client sees one number, has no visibility of what any package cost, and is negotiating a wrap on prices it has never seen competed. On a village this is usually a 20 to 30 per cent addition on a base the client cannot examine. 
FIVE PACKAGES, RUN TO THE END. Five tenders, five competitive prices, full visibility of every sum. What it buys is price. What it gives away is management: the ten interfaces at section 3 and the sixty statutory notice deadlines a year at 1.3 all sit with the client, and they sit with the same people who are running the main project. 
FIVE PACKAGES, CONSOLIDATED. Five competitive prices, fixed. Then one of the five, or a sixth party, takes the other four under it and the client holds one contract. What it buys is both — the price and the management — and what it costs is the wrap on prices the client has already seen, plus the transaction cost of the transfer. What it requires is that the day-one contracts allow it. 
The third route is the one this document is about, and the reason it is not the obvious answer everywhere is section 7: it is only available if somebody wrote four clauses at the start. 
4.2  When five is right, and when it is not 
Five is right when: the packages have genuinely different supply markets, so competing them separately reaches firms that would never appear under a single prime; the value is large enough that the competitive difference on each package exceeds the wrap; and the client has, or can buy, enough management capacity to hold the interfaces until it consolidates. 
Five is wrong when: two of the five are the same supply market and would be tendered to the same three firms; the total is small enough that five procurement exercises cost more than the price difference they find; or the client's management capacity is already committed to the main project, in which case the client is not choosing five packages, it is choosing to under-manage five packages, which is a different decision and a worse one. 
The honest test is not whether five is theoretically better. It is whether the client can name the person who will hold the ten interfaces, and whether that person has the time. If the answer is a name and a role that is already full, the client has chosen five and resourced one. 
4.3  The management load, counted rather than felt 
Consolidation is usually argued for on the grounds that five is 'too much'. That is true and it is not an argument, because it is not a number. Count it instead. 
With five packages the client holds: five contracts to administer; ten interfaces between packages; five interfaces between the accommodation and the main project; five monthly valuations; five sets of statutory payment dates; five change registers; five risk registers; five progress reports; and five sets of insurance, bonds and warranties to keep current. 
After consolidation the client holds: one contract; one interface with the accommodation; one valuation; one set of statutory dates; one change register; one risk register; one report. 
The ten internal interfaces do not disappear. They move inside the prime's scope and they are priced there. Anybody who tells a client that consolidation removes ten interfaces is selling something. It removes them from the client, which is worth paying for, and it charges for holding them, which is what the wrap at section 8 is.

5  When to consolidate, and when not to 
5.1  The trigger is a load problem, not a performance problem 
Consolidation is the right answer to a load problem and the wrong answer to a performance problem. The two feel identical from the client's chair and they have opposite remedies. 
A LOAD PROBLEM looks like this: every package is performing broadly to its contract, but the client cannot get to the interfaces. Coordination drawings are late because nobody has time to chair the meeting. A pay-less notice was nearly missed. The five progress reports arrive in five formats and nobody reconciles them. The person named as holding the accommodation is the same person holding the main project's mechanical package. Nothing is failing; everything is late by a fortnight in a way that compounds. That is what consolidation fixes. 
A PERFORMANCE PROBLEM looks like this: one of the five is not performing, and the client is spending its management effort on that one. Consolidating does not fix it. It transfers a failing contractor to a prime who will price the failure at its worst case, or refuse to take it, and the client will have paid a wrap to acquire a problem it still owns commercially. Deal with the failing package under its own contract first. Consolidate after, or consolidate the other four and keep that one direct. 
The test is simple and it is worth writing down before the decision is taken: IF EVERY PACKAGE PERFORMED PERFECTLY TOMORROW, WOULD THE CLIENT STILL BE OVERLOADED? If yes, the problem is the packaging and consolidation answers it. If no, the problem is a contractor and consolidation buries it. 
5.2  When to do it in the programme 
There is a window, and it is not the whole job. 
TOO EARLY — before the four have priced and been awarded — is not consolidation, it is single-prime procurement, and the client gives away the competitive prices it was trying to buy. 
TOO LATE — once the four are substantially complete — is paying a wrap for coordination that has already happened. What is left at that point is defects and operation, and those are better handled by a maintenance and operation contract than by a prime wrap over four finished builders. 
THE WINDOW opens when the last of the five is awarded and its price is fixed, and it closes when the second package reaches practical completion. Inside that window the client has what it went to five for — five competed prices — and still has something worth managing. 
One refinement, because it decides section 6: the earlier in the window, the more the prime is managing construction; the later, the more it is managing readiness and the transition into operation. That changes which of the five should be the prime.

6  The three mechanisms, and choosing the prime 
6.1  Three mechanisms, and what each actually is 
There are three, and they are routinely confused with one another. The differences matter because they have different costs, different timescales and different failure modes. 
MECHANISM A — CONSOLIDATION ONTO AN INCUMBENT. One of the five existing contractors is elevated to prime. The other four contracts are novated down to it, so that it stands in the client's shoes under each of them and the four become its subcontractors. Its own contract is varied to add the management and integration scope, the single-point accountability and a revised payment mechanism. 
MECHANISM B — NOVATION TO A NEW PRIME. A sixth party is appointed and all five contracts are novated to it. The client holds one contract with a party that was not part of the original competition. 
MECHANISM C — MANAGEMENT WRAP WITHOUT TRANSFER. A manager is appointed to coordinate the five. The five contracts stay with the client. The client still holds the contracts, the statutory notices and the risk; what it buys is somebody to run the meetings and chase the drawings. 
Mechanism C is not consolidation and should not be described as it. It reduces the client's effort and changes none of the client's exposure. It is the right answer when the enabling clauses are missing and a novation cannot be achieved, and it should be priced as a service, not as a prime wrap. 
6.2  Which one, and why A is usually the answer 
MECHANISM A is faster, cheaper and less disruptive than B, for three reasons that have nothing to do with the merits of the parties. 
FIRST, one fewer transfer. Under A the prime's own contract is varied, not novated; only four contracts move. Under B, five move, and the fifth is the largest. 
SECOND, the prime already knows the job. It has been on the project, in the meetings, and it has priced its own package against the same drawings. A new party has to learn the job and will price the learning as risk. 
THIRD, and this is the one that decides it: under A the transferee is a party the other four already work alongside, which is exactly what the named-party limitation at E2 was written to permit. Under B the transferee is a stranger to the four, and if the enabling clause limits the field to an existing project party — which is what makes the clause acceptable to a contractor in the first place — then B is not available without four consents freely given. 
Mechanism B earns its place when none of the five is capable of holding the others, or when the client wants the prime to be a party with no package of its own — see the conflict point at 6.3. 
6.3  Which of the five becomes the prime 
Not the biggest. The criteria are at the table below, and they pull in two directions. 
THE FM AND OPERATION PACKAGE has the strongest incentive alignment of the five, and by a distance. It is the only package whose scope runs the full deployment. It is the only one that interfaces with all four others in use rather than in construction. And it is the only one that has to live with the result: a contractor who will run the village for thirty-eight months has an interest in the four packages being right that no builder leaving at practical completion can have. Give the prime role to the party who cannot walk away from a bad job. 
THE MODULAR PACKAGE usually has the strongest capacity: the largest sum, the deepest balance sheet, the most construction management on the books, and the most credible claim to be able to carry four other contracts. It is also, on most villages, the package the other three are waiting on, which means it is already coordinating them informally. 
THE TIE-BREAK IS WHERE YOU ARE IN THE PROGRAMME. Consolidating mid-build, with two or more packages substantially incomplete, the work to be managed is construction and the modular contractor is the better prime. Consolidating once the build is broadly under control and the problem is readiness, handover and running the place, the FM and operation contractor is the better prime — and that is the more common trigger, because the load problem at 5.1 tends to bite as the packages converge on handover rather than while they are still apart. 
THE CONFLICT, STATED PLAINLY. Whichever of the five becomes prime is now managing four contracts and its own. It will be assessing its own progress, certifying its own work and holding the change register that its own variations go into. That is a real conflict and it is not a reason to abandon Mechanism A; it is a reason to keep the client's own certification of the prime's own package separate from the prime's certification of the other four. Clause 13.4 says how. 
6.4  Partial consolidation, which is often the honest answer 
The client does not have to move all four. Moving three and keeping one direct is a legitimate outcome and it is usually the right one in two cases. 
WHERE ONE OF THE FOUR IS UNDERPERFORMING. Per 5.1, a failing contractor should not be transferred. Keep it direct, deal with it under its own contract, and novate it later or not at all. 
WHERE ONE OF THE FOUR WILL NOT NOVATE AND CANNOT BE MADE TO. If the enabling clause is absent from that one contract, the client is negotiating from nothing. Do not pay a ransom to complete the set. Consolidate the three that will move, keep the fourth direct, and accept that the client retains one interface instead of none. Three of four is most of the benefit for none of the ransom. 
What the client must not do is let the prime price the wrap as if it were taking four, and then take three. The wrap is priced per package taken, and the price comes down when the count does.

7  The four clauses that make it possible at all 
Everything before this section is a decision. This section is drafting, and it happens eighteen months before the decision is taken. Without these four clauses in all five contracts, consolidation requires four consents given for the first time at the moment the client needs them — which is the moment each contractor has most leverage and least reason to give it cheaply. 
One of four refusing does not stop a consolidation. It makes it partial, and a partial consolidation is worse than either extreme unless it is chosen deliberately (see 6.4). 
 
The clause 
What it must say 
Why, and what happens without it 
The detail people get wrong 
E1 
The novation obligation 
The contract shall oblige the contractor, on the employer's written notice, to enter into a deed of novation in the form annexed to the contract, transferring the contract to a party the employer names, within a stated period of not more than twenty working days. 
Without it, novation requires the contractor's consent given for the first time at the moment the employer needs it — which is the moment the contractor has most leverage and least reason to give it cheaply. One of four refusing does not stop the consolidation; it makes the consolidation partial, which is worse than either extreme. 
The form of deed is annexed AT EXECUTION. A clause obliging a party to sign 'a deed in a form to be agreed' obliges them to nothing. 
E2 
The named-party limitation 
The employer may nominate as transferee any party which, at the date of the notice, holds a contract with the employer on the same project, or which meets stated financial and insurance criteria set out in the contract. 
It is the clause that makes E1 acceptable to a contractor. A bare obligation to novate to anyone the employer names is a covenant to accept an unknown counterparty, and a well-advised contractor will not give it. Limiting the field to an existing project party, or to a party meeting a stated credit test, is reasonable, is accepted, and — this is the point — still permits consolidation onto an incumbent. 
State the financial criteria as numbers: net asset position, professional indemnity limit, public liability limit. 'A party of equivalent standing' is not a criterion. 
E3 
The cut-off valuation mechanism 
The contract shall provide that on novation the works shall be valued at the date of transfer in accordance with a stated procedure, and that the sum so valued shall be conclusive as between employer and contractor as to work executed to that date, save for latent defects and any matter notified before the transfer. 
Novation without a cut-off leaves the transferred contract carrying an unvalued past. The new prime will not take on a liability it cannot see, so it will either price it at its worst case or refuse to take it. Both are expensive, and both are avoidable by valuing on a stated date under a procedure agreed before anyone had a reason to argue about it. 
It also fixes the four sums, which is what protects the competitive prices the client bought. Without it the prime will want the four to become provisional. 
E4 
The retention, bond and warranty carry-over 
The contract shall state what happens on novation to retention held, to any bond or parent company guarantee given to the employer, and to collateral warranties: which survive to the employer, which are re-issued to the transferee, and who bears the cost of any re-issue. 
A performance bond is a contract with a surety, and a surety is not bound by a novation it did not agree to. A bond that lapses on the day of transfer takes with it the only security the client had, and nobody notices until it is needed. Retention held by the employer for work now managed by a prime is either a debt the employer owes the prime, or a fund the employer still administers under a side agreement — and if the contract does not say which, both parties will assume the answer that suits them. 
Get the surety's consent in principle at the time the bond is taken out, not on the day of the transfer. 
7.5  If the clauses are not there 
The client is not without options, but it is without leverage, and it should price that honestly before it starts. 
FIRST, ask. A contractor with more work to win, or one that would rather deal with a prime it already knows than with a client whose attention is elsewhere, may novate for nothing. Ask all four before assuming any of them will resist. 
SECOND, buy it. A consent has a price and the price is usually smaller than it feels — an accelerated payment term, a release of retention, an uplift on a variation already in dispute. Decide the ceiling before the conversation, not during it. 
THIRD, take Mechanism C instead. A management wrap without transfer buys the client somebody to run the interfaces while leaving the contracts, the notices and the risk where they are. It is materially less valuable than a consolidation and it should be priced as a service rather than as a prime wrap. It is also the honest answer when the client cannot get the consents and will not pay a ransom for them. 
What the client must not do is pay a ransom to complete the set. Three of four transferred is most of the benefit; the fourth, bought at a price set by a contractor who knows the client has already moved the other three, is the worst trade in this document. 
 
8  Pricing the wrap 
8.1  What the prime is entitled to charge for, and what it is not 
The wrap on a consolidation is narrower than the wrap on a prime contract let from the start, and the reason is simple: the prime did not procure the four sums. It is being handed prices somebody else competed. A prime pricing a consolidation as though it were pricing a prime contract is charging for work it did not do, and the client should say so in those words. 
WHAT IT IS ENTITLED TO CHARGE FOR: managing and integrating four contracts it did not write; carrying the interface risk between them; standing behind their performance to the client; the cost of its own people doing that; and a return on the risk it has taken on. 
WHAT IT IS NOT ENTITLED TO CHARGE FOR: a procurement margin on sums it did not procure. An uplift on the four prices dressed as risk contingency, when the prices are fixed by the cut-off at E3. A margin on its own package twice — once in its original tender and again inside the wrap. 
Ask for the stack, line by line, and refuse a single percentage. A prime that will not show the stack is not pricing management; it is pricing what it thinks the client will pay. 
8.2  The stack, and the range to expect 
Present and negotiate it as four lines, not one number. The percentages are applied to the transferred value — the four fixed sums — and not to the prime's own package, which was already priced. 
On a consolidation of competed prices expect the LOWER HALF of each range. The upper half is defensible on a fast transfer, a remote site, or where one of the four is in difficulty and the prime is taking a known problem. 
8.3  The four things to refuse 
FIRST: any proposal that the four sums become provisional, subject to verification, or subject to the prime's re-measurement. That gives away the competitive prices the client bought five packages to get. The sums are fixed by the cut-off valuation at E3 and they stay fixed. 
SECOND: contingency without a joint risk register and a written drawdown process. Contingency in a prime stack is not profit and must not become it. Unused contingency is returned, shared on a stated basis, or retained only under a genuine fixed-price risk transfer — and the client should be offered the choice at the point of agreement rather than discovering the answer at the final account. 
THIRD: an open-ended indemnity for anything that arose before the transfer. The prime will ask, because it is taking on four unknowns. The answer is the cut-off at E3 plus a stated list of notified matters, not an indemnity. 
FOURTH: a wrap priced on four packages when only three are moving. See 5.4. 
8.4  What the arithmetic actually says, and why you should still probably do it 
The companion model to this document (consolidation-model.xlsx) computes the break-even wrap rate: the gross stack at which consolidation pays for itself on measurable expected value alone. On a village of the size worked in that model — four packages transferred at seventeen million, twenty-four months of client management avoided, and the delay risk halved — the break-even rate is TWO AND A HALF PER CENT. 
The market rate is seventeen to twenty-one. The gap is over three million pounds. 
That number is stated here rather than buried, because a client who has not done this arithmetic negotiates differently from one who has, and because a document that recommended a twenty-one per cent wrap without showing that its measurable return is two and a half would be selling rather than advising. 
WHAT THE GAP BUYS. Three things, none of which the model can price. Certainty: an expected value is not a cash-flow, and a client that cannot survive the bad outcome does not get to average across the distribution. Capacity: a management team of six, assembled, held for twenty-four months and accountable — the model prices the hours at an internal cost per hour, which assumes the people exist and are free, and on most projects they are neither. And accountability: single point, which is worth something the moment two of the four blame each other and the client is no longer the one standing between them. 
WHAT TO DO WITH IT. Go into the conversation at 8.2 knowing the break-even. Push hardest on the management and integration line, because it is the only one with a checkable headcount behind it: ask for the names, the grades and the time allocation, and multiply. Accept the overhead and profit lines, which are what they are. And treat contingency as returnable, because at four per cent of the transferred value it is the largest single number in the stack that the client can get back. 
Then pay the gap knowingly. That is a different act from paying it because nobody worked it out.

10  The eighteen traps 
Each of these has been the reason a consolidation cost more than it saved. None of them is exotic. Every one is avoidable by deciding it in writing before the transfer date rather than discovering it after. 
 
Kind 
The trap 
The answer 
T01 
Statutory 
A bond or parent company guarantee lapses on the day of novation because the surety never consented, and nobody notices until the security is needed. 
Obtain the surety's consent in principle when the bond is taken out, and its written consent before the transfer date. Treat an unconsented bond as no bond. 
T02 
Commercial 
Retention held by the client for work now managed by the prime is claimed by both and administered by neither. 
State in the deed whether it becomes a debt to the prime or stays with the client under a side agreement, and who certifies its release. 
T03 
Commercial 
Applications for payment in progress on the cut-off date are assessed twice, or not at all. 
Fix a cut-off date that falls between two valuation dates, not on one, and value everything to it under the E3 procedure. 
T04 
Legal 
A defect that arose before the transfer is disowned by the prime and no longer pursued by the client, because the contract it arose under has moved. 
The deed must expressly preserve the client's accrued rights to the cut-off, and list every matter already notified. 
T05 
Insurance 
The prime's professional indemnity aggregate, written for one package, is now the only cover behind five. 
Retest the limits against the enlarged scope before the transfer, and require evidence of the increased cover as a condition precedent to it. 
T06 
Statutory 
Everyone assumes the prime is now Principal Contractor under CDM 2015. Nobody has appointed it. 
Decide it, appoint it in writing, price it and check the prime's competence for the role. It is a legal duty, not a consequence of the word 'prime'. 
T07 
Tax 
The prime becomes a contractor for CIS purposes in respect of four subcontractors it did not verify. 
Verify all four before the first payment under the new arrangement, and check the prime's own gross payment status. A verification done late is a deduction taken at the higher rate. 
T08 
Tax 
The four now invoice the prime, not the client. Where both are CIS-registered the domestic reverse charge applies between them and the client's VAT float changes materially. 
Model the VAT cash effect before the transfer. It moves in the client's favour or against it depending on the client's own status, and either way it is a real number in the month it changes. 
T09 
Employment 
Nobody checks whether a service provision change arises under TUPE where any part of a service moves between providers. 
Check it. It is unlikely on a straight novation where the same firms continue, and it is not unlikely where the prime replaces a subcontractor's site team with its own. 
T10 
Programme 
The four transferred contracts each carry their own completion dates and damages. After transfer it is unclear whether they survive as between prime and subcontractor. 
State it. Usually they should survive unchanged, so that the prime inherits the leverage the client had rather than starting again. 
T11 
Commercial 
The client's liquidated damages, previously four separate caps against four parties, become one cap against one. 
Model it. Four contracts at five per cent each is a materially larger recovery than one contract at five per cent, and the prime will not volunteer the point. 
T12 
Commercial 
Price visibility is lost the day the four sums disappear into a single valuation. 
Make separate reporting of the four sums a condition of the variation, in every valuation, for the life of the contract. 
T13 
Governance 
The prime certifies its own package alongside the four it now manages. 
Keep the client's certification of the prime's own package separate and with the client. The prime certifies the four; the client certifies the prime. 
T14 
Risk 
Consolidation creates a single point of failure. The prime's insolvency is now five problems arriving together instead of one arriving alone. 
Reserve step-in rights to each of the four subcontracts, take direct collateral warranties from all four, and satisfy yourself about the prime's financial standing before, not after. 
T15 
Commercial 
The prime asks for the four sums to become provisional or subject to re-measurement. 
Refuse. See 8.3. This single point can give away everything five packages were let to achieve. 
T16 
Information 
Asset data, O&M information and as-built records that the client was receiving directly from four parties now arrive through the prime, in the prime's format, or not at all. 
Restate the information requirements in the variation, with the same schema and the same dates, and make them a condition of payment. 
T17 
Confidentiality 
Four contractors' prices and terms become visible to a fifth, who competes with at least one of them. 
Anticipate it in the enabling clause at E2 and deal with it in the deed: what the prime may see, what stays with the client, and a confidentiality undertaking from the prime to each of the four. 
T18 
Practical 
The novation completes and nobody tells the four's site teams, who carry on sending applications and RFIs to the client for another two months. 
A single communication on the transfer date to every named individual on all five contracts, stating who to send what to from that day, with the old route switched off rather than deprecated.

12  The deed of novation: what has to be in it 
This is an anatomy, not a precedent, and it is written so that a client can read a draft and see what is missing. The clause that decides who owns a defect discovered next year in work built last month is the fourth one, and it is the one most often adopted from a template rather than drafted. 
Clause 
What it does 
The point people miss 
Parties 
Client (transferor), original contractor, prime (transferee). All three. A two-party document is an assignment, and an assignment does not move the burden. 
Getting this wrong is the commonest drafting error and it leaves the client still liable under a contract it thinks it has transferred. 
Recitals 
The original contract, its date and value; the enabling clause relied on; the cut-off date; the decision to consolidate. 
The recitals are where the cut-off date is anchored. Every other clause refers back to it. 
The novation 
From the transfer date the prime is substituted for the client, and the contract continues as if the prime had always been a party — subject to the accrued-rights clause below. 
'As if it had always been a party' is the standard formulation and it is the one that transfers the burden. Understand it before agreeing it. 
Cut-off and accrued rights 
The valuation at the cut-off; the client's retained rights for anything arising before it; the schedule of notified matters. 
This is the clause that decides who owns a defect discovered next year in work built last month. Draft it, do not adopt it. 
Payment 
Sums certified and unpaid at cut-off — who pays them. Retention — who holds and who releases. The payment mechanism from the transfer date. 
Three separate questions that a single sentence will not answer. 
Securities 
Bond, PCG and warranty treatment, with the surety and guarantor consents annexed as conditions precedent. 
Annexed, not promised. A consent 'to be obtained' is a condition nobody chases after completion. 
Insurance 
The prime's evidence of cover at the enlarged limits, as a condition precedent to the transfer. 
Condition precedent, so the transfer does not take effect if the cover is not there. 
Confidentiality 
What the prime may see of the transferred contract, and an undertaking to each of the four. 
T17. It is what makes the transfer acceptable to a contractor whose competitor is now its client. 
Information 
The information, asset data and record requirements that continue, and to whom they are now delivered. 
T16. Restate them; do not assume they travel. 
Conditions precedent 
Insurance evidence, surety consents, CIS verification, CDM appointment where applicable, cut-off valuation agreed. 
Listed in one place so that the transfer either happens completely or does not happen. 
Three parties, always. A two-party document is an assignment; an assignment moves the benefit and leaves the burden behind, which means the client is still liable under a contract it believes it has transferred. It is the commonest drafting error in this whole exercise. 
 
13  After the transfer 
13.1  What the client still does 
Consolidation does not end the client's job; it changes it from coordination to assurance. Afterwards the client: certifies the prime, and only the prime; reads one valuation and checks the four sums reported separately under T12; holds one risk register jointly with the prime; audits the prime's management of the four rather than managing them itself; and keeps the step-in rights it reserved under T14 current and exercisable. 
What it stops doing is chairing interface meetings, reconciling five reports and serving sixty statutory notices a year. That is what it paid for. 
13.2  The first ninety days, where it goes wrong 
Three things fail in the first ninety days and all three are avoidable. 
COMMUNICATION. Site teams keep using the old routes. Applications arrive at the client, RFIs go to the wrong party, and two months later somebody discovers a request that nobody answered because everybody assumed somebody else had. Switch the old routes off on the transfer date. Not deprecated — off, with a redirect and a named person. 
THE FIRST VALUATION. The prime's first consolidated valuation is the one that establishes whether the arrangement works. Check it line by line: the four sums separately, the cut-off reconciled, the management fee on the transferred value and not on the whole, and the contingency shown as held rather than spent. Whatever is accepted in month one is the precedent for month thirty-eight. 
THE PRIME'S OWN PACKAGE. Per T13, the prime is now managing four contracts and performing a fifth. Watch the direction the pressure flows: a prime under pressure on its own package has four subcontractors whose programmes it controls. The client's separate certification of the prime's own work is the control, and it only works if the client actually does it. 
13.3  What it was worth, honestly 
The client pays the wrap at 8.2 on the transferred value, plus the transaction cost of the transfer — legal fees on five deeds, the cut-off valuations, and the management time of the twelve weeks at section 11. 
Against that it removes nineteen of the twenty coordination points at 4.3, fifty-nine of the sixty statutory notice deadlines, and the position of standing between ten interfaces with no contractual means of resolving any of them. 
What it does NOT do, and what nobody should claim it does: it does not make the interfaces go away, it does not make the four cheaper, and it does not transfer a risk the prime has priced at less than it is worth. It buys management, at a price, from a party who can do it. Judged against the alternative of buying the same management as a single prime tender at the start — with no competed prices behind it — it is the better trade, and it is the better trade by roughly the difference between five competitive prices and one negotiated one. 
That difference is the reason to plan for this at day one rather than arrive at it in month eighteen. Section 7 is a page of drafting. It is the cheapest page in the whole procurement. 
13.4  The separation that keeps it honest 
One rule, and it is the rule that makes Mechanism A defensible. 
THE PRIME CERTIFIES THE FOUR. THE CLIENT CERTIFIES THE PRIME. 
The prime assesses, values and certifies the four transferred subcontracts, because that is the management it is being paid for and it cannot be paid for it while the client still does it. 
The client assesses, values and certifies the prime — including, separately and visibly, the prime's own original package, which is not part of what the prime manages and must never be assessed by the party performing it. 
Every reporting line, every valuation format and every change register should be built on that split. Where the two are blurred, the conflict at 6.3 stops being a manageable feature of the structure and becomes the reason the structure failed.
