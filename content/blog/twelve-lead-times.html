Ask a mobilisation team what is holding up the start and you will usually get an answer about design, or planning, or the weather. Ask them six months later what actually held it up and the answer is almost always something that had a lead time nobody established.

This post does not give you lead times. Anybody quoting generic durations for these is guessing, because every one of them depends on your location, your supplier, your season and the state of the local supply chain on the day you ask. What it gives you is the list of twelve things whose lead time you have to establish yourself, in writing, before the date on the programme means anything — and the question to ask for each one.

The twelve

  1. The grid connection. Almost always the longest, almost always assumed to be shorter. Ask: what is the quoted connection date, is it conditional, and what is the application-to-energisation history for this distribution network operator in this area?
  2. The temporary supply, if there is no permanent one. A different application, a different queue and a different answer from the connection above. Ask both separately or you will be told about one.
  3. Generation, if there is neither. Sets are not the constraint; the right sets at the right rating with the right fuel and emissions standard can be. Ask: what is available at this rating, in this region, in this season.
  4. Water supply and wastewater discharge. Two separate consents from two separate parties, and the discharge one is routinely discovered late. Ask who consents it and what they need from you.
  5. Cabins and modular accommodation. Stock availability moves with the market and with the season. Ask: what is available now, what is available at the specification you actually want, and what is the difference in weeks between those two answers.
  6. Planning conditions attaching to the compound. Construction traffic management, working hours, lighting, noise and screening. Ask when each condition has to be discharged and who discharges it — this is a local authority timescale, not yours.
  7. Highway and access permissions. Section agreements, road openings, temporary traffic regulation orders, abnormal load routes. Each has its own authority and its own notice period.
  8. Land agreements. Leases, licences, wayleaves and temporary occupation. Ask who the signatory is and whether they have signed anything like it before — a landowner who has not is a longer conversation than one who has.
  9. Security systems. Not the guards; the systems. CCTV, access control, turnstiles and the connectivity behind them. Ask what the survey-to-commissioning duration is, because the survey cannot happen before the layout is fixed.
  10. Connectivity. A fixed line where one is available, and the fallback where it is not. This is the one most often assumed to be instant and most often is not.
  11. Waste arrangements. Registered carriers, permitted sites, and the duty of care paperwork. Usually short, occasionally not, and it stops the site if it is missing.
  12. Welfare that meets the standard from day one. Not the final arrangement — the one covering the people who build the compound. The minimum in {{source:cdmSchedule2|Schedule 2 of CDM 2015}} applies to the operatives laying the hardstanding, weeks before anyone occupies an office.

The pattern in the failures

Almost none of these fail because somebody forgot the item. They fail because the lead time was taken from the last project.

That is a reasonable-looking shortcut and it is wrong in a specific way: these durations are not properties of the item, they are properties of the place and the moment. A connection application in one region behaves nothing like the same application in another. Cabin stock in March is not cabin stock in September. A landowner who has signed four wayleaves signs the fifth in a fraction of the time.

The second pattern is sequencing. Several of these cannot start until something else is fixed. The security survey needs the layout. The layout needs the land agreement. The land agreement needs the route. A schedule that lists twelve lead times in parallel, when four of them are in series, produces a start date that was never achievable.

What to do with the list

Take it to the person who owns the mobilisation date and ask, for each of the twelve: who told us that number, when, and is it written down?

  • A named supplier or authority, dated, in writing — that is a lead time.
  • "About six weeks" from memory — that is an assumption, and it belongs on the risk register with an owner and a date by which it will be confirmed.
  • Nobody — that is the one that moves your start date.

The exercise takes an afternoon. It is worth doing before the programme is issued rather than after, because the only thing more expensive than an unknown lead time is a committed date built on one.
