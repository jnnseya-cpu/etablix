"Single point of responsibility" is one of those phrases that means something precise to the person saying it and something larger to the person hearing it. When we use the word prime, we mean one contract for the whole temporary scope. We do not mean that a statutory duty has moved, and the difference is worth ten minutes of anybody's time before it is written into an appointment.

Here is what prime covers, what it does not, and how it sits beside the other two ways of buying the same scope.

What a prime service contract actually is

It is a commercial arrangement. One contract, one programme, one invoice route and one party answerable for the whole temporary site environment — compounds and welfare, temporary power, water and drainage, security and fencing, logistics and deliveries, workforce accommodation where the site needs it, and the removal and reinstatement at the end. The supply chain sits behind us and you deal with us.

That is a procurement decision about how many contracts you want to manage. It is not a decision about who carries duties under the construction regulations, and it cannot be.

What it is not

{{source:cdm2015|The Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015}} require a client to appoint a principal contractor in writing where more than one contractor is or is likely to be involved on a project. That appointment carries duties for planning, managing and monitoring the construction phase of the whole project, and the {{source:hseCdmSummary|HSE's summary of duties}} sets out what those are.

We do not take that appointment. Where we work under a prime arrangement we are a contractor operating within the appointed principal contractor's arrangements, and the appointment says so in terms rather than by implication.

The reason to be blunt about it is that the confusion runs in a dangerous direction. A client who believes a duty has transferred because one supplier now holds a large scope will stop doing the things the duty requires. Nobody discovers that until something has gone wrong, and by then the question is not commercial.

Three ways to buy the same scope

The scope of work is identical in all three. What changes is who carries the contracts — and you choose, rather than us.

  • Advisory. We write the requirements, the specification and the tender documents. You run the procurement and you hold every contract. Our involvement ends when the documents are issued. Nothing joins your structure and nothing sits between you and your suppliers.
  • Management integrator. You contract every supplier directly and pay them directly. We control delivery across them: the programme, the interfaces, the sequencing, the thing that goes wrong on a Tuesday. We hold the responsibility and none of the money.
  • Prime service contractor. One contract for the whole temporary scope, with the supply chain behind us.

{{link:models|The three models}} are a ladder of involvement, and the lightest rung is a document. A client who wants their own team to keep every relationship should buy advisory and nothing else; a client whose constraint is management attention rather than knowledge should look at the middle rung first.

The money rule, which is the part most worth reading

Under management integrator, we never hold your supply-chain money. Every supplier is contracted by you and paid by you. We hold no retention, no margin on the trades and no payment obligation to anyone in the chain.

This is a rule rather than a preference, and the reasoning is about your programme rather than our balance sheet. An intermediary that holds a chain of suppliers' money concentrates the risk of its own solvency onto your site: if that firm fails, the suppliers stop, and they stop at the point in the programme when you can least absorb it. Keeping the contracts with you removes the question rather than answering it.

Payment in a construction supply chain is also not a matter of custom. {{source:hgcra|Part II of the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996}} sets out the payment and adjudication framework that applies to construction contracts, and a chain with fewer links in it is a chain with fewer places for a payment dispute to start.

Where prime is the right answer

Prime earns its place where the interfaces are the problem. A site with eleven separate suppliers and no single programme between them does not have a procurement problem; it has eleven relationships competing for the attention of somebody who is also building the permanent works.

It is the wrong answer where a client has the management capacity and simply wants the scope written down properly. That is advisory, it is cheaper, and we will say so.

What this means for your appointment

If you are considering any of the three, the things to settle before signing are the same three every time: which model, who holds which contracts, and what the appointment says about the principal contractor role. The third one takes a sentence and prevents the misunderstanding this whole piece is about.
