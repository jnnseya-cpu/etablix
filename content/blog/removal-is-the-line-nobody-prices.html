Every site establishment has two halves. The first is well understood: get the compound in, get the people welfare, get power and water and access, get the job started. The second half is taking all of it away again, putting the land back, and proving you did.

The first half is priced at bid stage by somebody who is thinking about it. The second is priced by the same person, in the same afternoon, as a percentage — and then not thought about again for two years, by which point the people who set the number have moved on and the people holding it were never asked.

Why it goes wrong, and it is not laziness

Reinstatement is the only part of the establishment where the scope is defined by somebody outside the project. A landowner, a highway authority, a planning condition or a lease decides what "back to how it was" means, and they decide it at the end, looking at the field, not at the start, looking at the specification.

Three things follow from that, and all three are structural rather than anybody's fault.

  • The scope is not knowable from the drawings. It is knowable from the pre-commencement condition survey, the planning conditions and the land agreements — documents that frequently sit with three different people, none of whom is the estimator.
  • The cost is incurred when the job has no money left. Retention is the last thing released, the account is being closed, and the team that would have argued about it has been redeployed.
  • There is no leverage at the end. At bid stage you can negotiate a reinstatement standard. At handover you are negotiating with somebody who has watched lorries on their lane for two years and wants their field back the way it was.

The wind-down is a programme, not an event

On most schemes the compound is treated as something that exists until it does not. In practice it comes down in stages, and the stages have to be sequenced against work that is still happening.

This is most acute on projects with a long commissioning tail — process plant, energy infrastructure, anything first-of-a-kind. Construction is nominally complete, the headcount has dropped, and the instinct is to strike the compound. But commissioning, testing and optimisation still need people on site, and those people still need the welfare minimum set out in {{source:cdmSchedule2|Schedule 2 of CDM 2015}}. A compound sized and programmed for the construction peak, then removed on the construction completion date, leaves a commissioning team improvising for months.

The opposite failure costs money rather than dignity: holding the full compound, fully serviced, for a workforce a fifth of its peak, because nobody built a wind-down sequence and the hire agreements ran on.

What a reinstatement scope actually contains

Not "make good". That phrase is where the argument starts. A scope that survives handover names, for each parcel or area:

  1. The pre-commencement condition, evidenced — photographs dated, a written survey, and ideally the landowner's countersignature on it. This is the single most valuable document in the entire process and it costs almost nothing to produce on day one.
  2. The standard of return, in words that can be tested. Topsoil depth and provenance, compaction, drainage restored, surfacing specification, fencing to the original line and specification, seeding or planting, and the season in which it has to be done.
  3. What is removed and what stays, item by item. Hardstanding, access tracks, culverts, ducts, fencing, gates. Some of it the landowner will want to keep; that is a negotiation that is worth money and is only available if it happens early.
  4. Waste routes and evidence. Where each material goes, under what duty of care, with transfer notes retained.
  5. Who signs it off, and what they are signing. A reinstatement accepted by nobody in particular is a reinstatement that can be reopened.

The commercial point

Reinstatement is the one line in the establishment where doing the work early is cheaper than doing it well later. The condition survey, the agreed standard of return and the wind-down sequence are all bid-stage documents that cost days. The dispute they prevent costs weeks, and it lands at the moment the project is least able to absorb it.

It is also the part of a project a client remembers. A compound that came out cleanly, on a field that looks like a field, is the last impression a contractor leaves with a landowner, a parish council and a planning officer — all three of whom will be consulted the next time somebody wants to build something nearby.

The question to ask at bid stage

Who has seen the land, and what did they write down?

If the answer is a dated condition survey with photographs and a named person, reinstatement has been priced. If the answer is a percentage, it has been deferred — and the person it has been deferred to does not know yet.
