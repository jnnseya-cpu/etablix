A substation is one compound. You establish it once, you hold it for two years or more, and every pound you spend on it is amortised across the whole job. Almost every preliminaries model in British construction was built for that shape, because almost all the experience behind those models came from sites like it.

A cable route, an overhead line, a pipeline or a highways scheme is not that shape at all. It is dozens of short-duration sites that move along a corridor, each with a different landowner, a different access, a different distance to the nearest working face and a different reinstatement obligation. The establishment does not get spent once. It gets spent again every time the work moves.

What actually changes

The list of things you need does not change much. What changes is how many times you need them, for how long, and what it costs to put them somewhere.

  • The compound recurs. Set up, service, take down, reinstate, and do it again a few kilometres later. Each cycle carries mobilisation cost that a fixed compound pays once.
  • Land is held by agreement, not possession. Wayleaves, temporary occupation agreements and licences come with conditions on where you can stand, what you can store, how long you can stay and what condition you return it in. A compound on land you do not possess is a compound somebody else can constrain.
  • Welfare travel becomes a productivity line. Facilities that meet the standard but sit fifteen minutes from the working face cost two journeys a day for every operative, every day. That is rarely measured and never recovered.
  • There is frequently no supply of anything. No mains power, no water, no drainage, no telecoms. Everything is generated, tankered or carried, and every one of those is a vehicle movement on somebody's lane.
  • The perimeter cannot be fenced. Security on a corridor is a patrol and a set of compounds, not a line. It is a different service bought from a different kind of supplier.
  • Reinstatement is per parcel. Every landowner has their own standard, their own sensitivities and their own memory of what the field looked like. One dissatisfied farmer on a twenty-kilometre route is a disproportionate problem.

The welfare question is a legal one, not a logistical one

The obligation does not soften because the site is difficult. {{source:cdmSchedule2|Schedule 2 of the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015}} sets a minimum list, and a remote or moving site is still a construction site. What a linear scheme changes is what is reasonably practicable in the arrangements, not whether the arrangements are required.

The HSE's guidance on {{source:hseWelfare|welfare on construction sites}} recognises mobile and relocatable provision for exactly this reason. The harder question, and the one that decides whether people actually use the facilities, is travel time from the working face. Mobile welfare that follows the work is a different procurement from static cabins delivered once, and it needs the moves to be on the programme rather than arranged the week before.

Why the number comes out wrong

Establishment cost per pound of construction value runs materially higher on linear work than on a fixed compound. That is not an opinion about anyone's estimating: it is what happens when a cost that is normally spent once is spent fifteen times, and when the thing being established has to be carried to places with no services.

The failure is usually a rate. Somebody takes a preliminaries percentage from the last job — a substation, a building, a plant — and applies it to a corridor. The percentage is not wrong for the job it came from. It is simply describing a different shape of site.

The second failure is subtler and more expensive. On a fixed compound the establishment is a cost. On a linear scheme it is a programme constraint, because the work cannot move until the next compound is ready, and the next compound cannot be ready until the land agreement, the access, the power and the reinstatement of the last one are all resolved. Compound moves sit on the critical path far more often than they appear on the programme.

What to do instead

  1. Model it per parcel, not per scheme. How many compounds, where, for how long, with what access, serving how many people at what distance. The total falls out of that. It does not fall out of a percentage.
  2. Put the compound moves on the programme. With durations, and with the land agreement and the reinstatement of the previous parcel as predecessors. If a move is not on the programme it is not managed.
  3. Price reinstatement at the start, per landowner. It is the line most often left to the end, and the end is when you have the least leverage and the most impatience.
  4. Buy the recurring services once. Welfare, security, waste and generation across the whole route from one arrangement, not per section. Fifteen small enquiries into one regional supply chain is fifteen chances to pay the small-order price.
  5. Measure welfare travel time. It is the cheapest number to collect and one of the few establishment decisions that pays back daily.

The one question worth asking at bid stage

Whoever is pricing the corridor: how many times does the compound move, and is every one of those moves on the programme?

If the answer is a number and the moves are on the programme, the establishment has been thought about. If the answer is a percentage, it has been inherited from a site that did not move.
