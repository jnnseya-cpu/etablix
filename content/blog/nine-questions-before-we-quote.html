We are asked for a ballpark most weeks, and the request is entirely reasonable. Somebody has a board paper due, or a feasibility decision to make, and they want to know whether this is a hundred thousand pound problem or a million pound one.

The honest answer is that a number given without the nine answers below is a guess wearing a decimal point. And a guess given to a client does not stay a guess: it goes into a cost plan, it gets repeated to a board, and six months later it is the figure our actual price is judged against — with the gap attributed to us rather than to the information that was missing when it was given.

So here are the nine. None of them is difficult. Several of them are not written down anywhere, which is the point.

The nine

  1. What is the peak headcount, and what is the curve getting there? Not the number on day one. The busiest twelve weeks decide the size of everything.
  2. How long is the programme, including demobilisation? Every recurring line is multiplied by this, and the removal period is routinely left off the end.
  3. Are mains water, drainage and power available at the boundary? If not, what is the distance and whose land is in between.
  4. What are the access and turning constraints? The largest vehicle that has to reach the compound decides the hardstanding and often the layout.
  5. What planning or consent conditions attach to the temporary works? Hours, routes, lighting, noise, hoarding, reinstatement. These are conditions, not preferences.
  6. Is the workforce local, or does it need accommodation? This is the single largest branch in the whole estimate.
  7. What is the shift pattern, and does it overlap? Two shifts meeting for thirty minutes is a different canteen from two shifts that do not.
  8. Who holds the principal contractor appointment? Under {{source:cdm2015|CDM 2015}} that appointment is the client's to make, and the {{source:hseCdmSummary|duties that follow it}} shape who arranges what.
  9. What is the reinstatement standard at the end? "As found" and "to the satisfaction of the landowner" are different numbers, and one of them is unbounded.

Why the questions are the deliverable

Here is the part worth noticing. On most projects we can assemble the answers in about a week — where the information exists and somebody can point at it. The constraint is almost never analysis. It is that two or three answers are not recorded anywhere, and finding them means asking a utility company, a planning officer or whoever owns the programme.

Those are also, reliably, the answers that take longest to act on. A temporary supply sits in a distribution network operator's queue whatever your programme says. A condition you discover in month four was in the consent all along. The questions that are slow to answer are slow to fix, and they are the ones that move a start date.

So asking them early is not diligence for its own sake. It is the cheapest programme protection available, and it costs a week.

When we will give you a range

Where a client genuinely needs an order of magnitude for a feasibility decision, we will give one — and we will say what drives the range and how wide it is, which is usually questions 3 and 6. What we will not do is give a single figure that looks like a price, because the person who receives it will reasonably treat it as one.
