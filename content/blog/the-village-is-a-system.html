Here is a compound we were asked to look at. Every unit on the order was the right unit. The welfare met the standard. The offices were the correct size. The canteen had the right number of seats for the headcount.

It still did not work. The drying room was at the far end from the entrance, so wet clothing travelled through the canteen twice a day. The pedestrian route to the toilets crossed the delivery route. And the single thirty-minute break put eighty people into a canteen sized for eighty people, which sounds correct until you watch the queue.

Nothing was bought wrongly. The establishment was still wrong, because nothing on an order form describes what happens between the units.

A list buys objects. A system buys an arrangement.

An order specifies things: six units, this specification, that delivery date. An establishment specifies an operating environment — the things, plus everything between them.

  • Where people walk, and whether it crosses where vehicles turn.
  • Which units share a drainage run, and whether that run exists.
  • How far the furthest operative travels to reach a toilet, twice a day, every day.
  • What the peak headcount is, as opposed to the headcount in week one.
  • What happens when the site grows, and where the second phase goes.
  • The order units arrive in, and whether the last one has crane access.

The units are the cheapest part of the decision. The arrangement is the expensive one, and it is the part that arrives with no price against it.

Size it against the curve, not the day

The single commonest sizing error is using the mobilisation headcount instead of the peak. A compound sized for the first trade is undersized for the busiest twelve weeks, and expanding it later costs a second mobilisation, a second set of connections and a second disruption to a site that is by then fully occupied.

The inputs are not difficult to obtain: the headcount curve, the shift pattern and its overlap, travel distance to facilities, and whether the site is split into working areas that each need their own provision. They are simply not usually asked for, because the question arrives as "how many units do we need" rather than "what does this site look like in month nine".

The standards set a floor, not a layout

{{source:cdm2015|CDM 2015}} requires suitable and sufficient welfare facilities to be provided and maintained throughout the construction phase, and the {{source:workplaceAcop|approved code of practice on workplace health, safety and welfare}} is where the underlying standards for a fixed workplace sit. Both tell you what has to exist. Neither tells you where to put it.

Vehicle and pedestrian separation is the one interface where the gap between "compliant" and "well arranged" is not merely a productivity question. The HSE's guidance on {{source:hseSiteTransport|construction site transport safety}} is explicit that keeping people and vehicles apart is a planning decision taken before the site opens, not a signage decision taken afterwards.

Where villages actually fail

In our experience, almost never in a component. The failures cluster at the interfaces:

  • Drainage that assumed a mains connection and found none.
  • A power requirement that exceeded the temporary supply once heating was switched on in November.
  • Pedestrian and vehicle routes planned by two different people.
  • Canteen capacity that worked for the headcount but not for one thirty-minute break.
  • A delivery sequence that stranded the last unit.

Each of those is a failure to plan the objects as one thing. None of them is visible on an order.

Designed as one thing, delivered by many

Several suppliers can deliver the parts, and usually do — that is not the argument. What cannot be split is the arrangement: one layout, one services strategy, one headcount curve, one delivery and removal sequence, and one person answerable when two of those disagree.

Where the arrangement is split, the interfaces become nobody's work.
