A compliance gate plus a price score awards the contract to whichever tenderer read your specification least carefully. That is not a cynical observation, it is arithmetic: if compliance is pass or fail and price is the only thing that varies, then every assumption a bidder leaves out is worth money to them and costs you nothing until month three.

Here is a scoring model for a site-services package that does not do that, and the four questions that separate a real bid from an optimistic one.

Why the cheapest bid usually is not

Site services are priced in three parts, and only one of them is visible at tender.

  • Capital or hire. The units, the plant, the installation. Easy to compare, and the part everybody compares.
  • Operating. Servicing, waste, consumables, cleaning, fuel, breakdown response. A monthly figure multiplied by a duration that is not fixed yet, so it is where an optimistic assumption hides best.
  • Exit. Off-hire, removal, reinstatement, dilapidations. Frequently absent from the bid entirely, and the hardest to argue about later because by then the tender is a year old.

A bid that is cheapest on part one and silent on parts two and three is not a cheaper bid. It is a shorter one.

The scoring model

Weightings are a client decision and they should be set before the bids arrive, not after. What follows is the shape we use on advisory work, with the reasoning, so you can argue with the numbers rather than inherit them.

  • Whole-life cost — 50. Not the capital line. Capital plus operating across the programmed duration plus exit, on one arithmetic basis you publish. Every bidder prices the same duration and the same assumed headcount curve, supplied by you, so the comparison is of prices rather than of assumptions.
  • Deliverability against the programme — 20. Lead times named per item, resource named, and the interfaces they depend on identified. A bidder who tells you their connection depends on your access road is a bidder who has read the programme.
  • Operating discipline — 15. Servicing frequencies, response times, escalation, and who holds the record. This is the part that decides what the site is like in month nine, and it is almost never scored.
  • Compliance and competence — 10. Scored, not gated. A gate tells you who cleared the bar; a score tells you who is well clear of it, which is different information.
  • Social value and local content — 5. Only where it is evidenceable. An unevidenceable commitment scored at 20 corrupts the whole model, because it is free to promise.

The four questions that separate the bids

Put these in the tender documents as required responses, not as clarifications afterwards. A clarification round is a delay you pay for; a required response is information you specified.

  1. What is your price if the duration extends by eight weeks? You want the rate, not a promise to be reasonable. Programmes move.
  2. What is your price if peak headcount is twenty per cent higher? The whole establishment is sized off one number, and that number is an estimate on the day you issue.
  3. What is excluded, listed? A required exclusions schedule turns the thing a bidder was hoping you would not notice into a document they signed.
  4. What does removal and reinstatement cost, and to what standard? Priced at tender it is a line. Priced at closeout it is a negotiation you enter without leverage.

Make the pack priceable before it goes out

Most bad evaluations are caused earlier, at the define stage. If the scope and the pricing schedule were written separately, they will not reconcile: a scope item with no priced line comes back as a query or as a qualification, and a priced line naming a scope item that does not exist comes back as an argument.

We check this by machine before a pack is issued, and the check refuses the issue rather than warning about it. Every scope item carries a reference, every priced line names one, and the two sets are compared. It is a small control with a large effect, because it converts a month-three dispute into a five-minute fix at issue. The same discipline is what makes a tender comparable at all: {{link:offer|the defined output of each product}} sets out where scope, pricing and evaluation sit in an engagement.

Where public procurement changes this

On public work the {{source:procurementAct|Procurement Act 2023}} governs how the requirement must be described and how an award is made, and the practical consequence is upstream of the evaluation: the package has to be described well enough to be assessed against published criteria before anybody prices it. Getting the define stage wrong is not just commercially expensive there, it is procedurally awkward.

Payment is worth naming in the same breath. Part II of the {{source:hgcra|Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996}} sets the payment mechanism for construction contracts, and a scoring model that ignores payment terms is one that quietly rewards a bidder planning to fund themselves from your supply chain. {{link:supply|Our own payment discipline is published}}, because a supplier deciding whether to price your work is running this same evaluation on you.

A worked example, invented on purpose

Invented deliberately. Construction is small enough that a real project is identifiable from its constraints alone, so nothing here is drawn from a live engagement.

Three bids for a 200-person welfare compound over 60 weeks. Bid A is lowest on capital and prices no exit. Bid B is 9% higher on capital, prices exit, and its operating rate is a third below the other two. Bid C is highest on capital, prices exit at a defensible number, and its operating rate is in line. On a capital-only comparison A wins. On whole-life at the programmed duration, B wins — until you ask question one and discover B's operating rate assumes a servicing frequency below what the site will need, which is why it is a third cheaper. C wins on the model above, and it wins on the arithmetic rather than on anybody's judgement, which is the point.

What this does not solve

A scoring model does not make a bad shortlist good, and it does not protect you from a bidder who prices correctly and then performs poorly. It also has a cost: whole-life scoring needs a duration and a headcount curve you are prepared to publish, and some clients would rather not commit to either at tender. That is a legitimate position, but the consequence is that you are buying part one of three and finding out about the rest later.

Nor does it remove the judgement. Somebody competent still has to decide whether a servicing frequency is adequate, and that is not an arithmetic question.
